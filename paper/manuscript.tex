\begin{abstract}
Circular knitting machines are widely used in continuous fabric production. Persistent needle or yarn abnormalities can generate repeated fabric defects, causing material waste and economic losses. To enable in-process inspection and intervention, we design and implement a dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system and develop a lightweight detector termed \model. The system combines global-shutter imaging and LED illumination, a coaxial SerDes camera link, a self-developed RK3566 mainboard, and a power adapter supporting 100--500 V AC input. The mainboard integrates image reception, LED brightness control, machine input/output interfaces, and 4G communication. A Hall-sensor rotation signal activates acquisition and inspection. When a defect is detected, the system issues a local wired stop command, uploads the defect record, and pushes an alert to the worker for corrective intervention. To explicitly incorporate the contrasting morphologies of elongated defects and localized holes, morphology-aware context encoding (MCE) couples extended structural context with local defect evidence. To accommodate variations in defect morphology and scale, gated interpolation upsampling (GIU) adapts the reconstruction of higher-level context before fusion with local features, using a compact content-dependent gate. On the test set of a self-collected dataset from a real circular-knitting production line, \model achieves 97.2\% mAP50 and 65.5\% mAP50--95, improving on the YOLOv8n baseline by 6.7 and 7.1 percentage points, respectively. Deployed on the RK3566, it achieves an inference throughput of 22 frames/s and is integrated into the local wired stop-control workflow. The cloud platform supports defect reporting and worker notification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines are important production infrastructure for high-volume weft-knit fabrics used in T-shirts and sportswear \cite{cottonworksKnitBasics,woolmarkCircularKnitting,cottonworksSingleDouble}. The International Textile Manufacturers Federation reported approximately 24,500 global shipments of large circular knitting machines in 2025 \cite{itmf2025press}. Globally, approximately 92 million tonnes of textile waste are generated annually, highlighting the importance of reducing avoidable material losses \cite{unep2025textilewaste}. Conventional quality checks are often performed after knitting is completed. A persistent needle or yarn abnormality can therefore generate repeated defects along a fabric roll before discovery, accumulating substantial fabric waste and economic losses \cite{takeuchi2018knitted,akter2022material,xiang2023hooknet}. Real-time detection during knitting is needed to identify these defects and enable intervention before further defective fabric is produced.

Edge computing has been increasingly adopted for industrial defect inspection. In textile applications, Song et al. deployed EfficientDet-D0 on an NVIDIA Jetson TX2 and evaluated five fabric datasets covering dark red, grid, camouflage, digitally printed, and light blue fabrics, without specifying the fabric-formation process \cite{song2021edgefabric}. Machado et al. deployed YOLO11n on a Jetson Orin Nano to inspect solid-color cotton and linen fabrics on a fabric rolling line \cite{machado2025edgefabric}. Yan et al. deployed an improved YOLOv5s model on a Huawei Ascend 310-based Atlas platform to inspect warp-knitted lace on warp-knitting machines \cite{yan2026warplace}.

Beyond textile inspection, Wang et al. deployed an improved YOLOv5s model on a Jetson Xavier NX for rail surface defect detection \cite{wang2024rail}. Li et al. deployed LiteYOLO-ID on a Jetson TX2 NX for insulator defect detection \cite{li2024liteyolo}, and Zhao et al. implemented KAM-Net on a Xilinx ZCU102 FPGA for glass defect segmentation \cite{zhao2025kamnet}. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:hardware-platforms}, these studies primarily rely on general-purpose edge-computing platforms or FPGA development boards. Such platforms are not specifically designed for the sensing, control, and installation requirements of circular knitting machines. Their hardware costs, together with the additional integration required for image acquisition and machine control, can limit the economic feasibility of deployment across large numbers of machines. These limitations motivate a dedicated, low-cost inspection system tailored to circular knitting production.

At the algorithm level, YOLO-based detectors have been widely explored for fabric defect detection because of their efficient inference and explicit defect localization \cite{redmon2016yolo}. Kang and Li proposed AYOLOv7-tiny for solid-color circular weft fabrics, combining space-to-depth downsampling with hybrid attention to preserve subtle defect information \cite{kang2024ayolov7}. Yang et al. developed YOLOV4-TinyS using lightweight convolutions, shallow-feature fusion, and attention, and evaluated the detector in a conveyor-based fabric inspection system \cite{yang2024yolov4tinys}. These studies demonstrate the importance of preserving fine defect details while reducing computational complexity.

More recent fabric detectors have focused on context modeling and multiscale feature fusion. FA-YOLO combines receptive-field enhancement, attention, and adaptive spatial feature fusion \cite{yu2024fayolo}. Chen et al. enhanced YOLOv8 with Swin Transformer blocks, a bidirectional feature pyramid, attention, and a modified localization loss \cite{chen2025yolov8}. DGHR-YOLO integrates deformable convolution for morphology adaptation with a high-level screening feature pyramid and lightweight feature processing \cite{zhang2025dghr}. Together, these methods advance the representation of subtle defects, irregular shapes, and scale variation. Related work on photovoltaic-cell and pipeline-weld inspection also explores task-specific feature pyramids, contextual relations, and lightweight rotated representations \cite{su2022baf,shen2024crnet,liu2025lrnet}.

Despite this progress, adapting fabric detectors to in-process inspection on circular knitting machines requires joint consideration of defect morphology and deployment constraints. Prior work already addresses circular weft fabrics, scale variation, and irregular shapes. For the defect patterns considered here, a remaining design question is how to jointly represent the extended continuity of elongated defects and the localized texture disruption of holes against repetitive knitted backgrounds. These complementary forms of evidence must also remain spatially informative when coarse features are reconstructed during multiscale fusion. Explicitly incorporating these morphology priors into a compact feature representation is therefore a central requirement of our detector. The resulting operator graph must be evaluated on the target RK3566 platform, since parameter counts and floating-point operation counts alone do not establish inference latency.

These requirements motivate explicit use of defect morphology as an architectural prior. Elongated defects require context that links responses over their spatial extent, whereas localized holes require concentrated evidence of a local texture disruption. We therefore combine spatially extended context with retained local feature paths and adapt feature reconstruction to the feature content during multiscale fusion. This design targets defect continuity, local appearance, and spatial fidelity as complementary representation requirements under a limited edge-computing budget. At the system level, the detector must operate alongside reliable image acquisition and local machine control so that recognizing a defect can lead directly to intervention during knitting.

To this end, we first design and implement a dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system for circular knitting machines. The system integrates a power adapter, a global-shutter camera with LED illumination, a coaxial SerDes image-transmission link, a Hall sensor, and a self-developed RK3566 edge board. The rotation signal activates image acquisition and detection. When a defect is detected, the edge device issues a local wired stop command, uploads the defect image and record, and uses the cloud platform to push an alert to the worker for corrective intervention. Local inference and machine-stop control connect defect recognition to production control, while cloud communication supports reporting and worker notification \cite{shi2016edge}.

For defect recognition within this system, we develop \model (morphology-aware context and gated interpolation), a lightweight detector based on YOLOv8n that explicitly incorporates priors about defect continuity and localized texture disruption. MCE supplies spatially extended context while retaining the original local C2f feature paths for holes and fine structures. GIU addresses the different reconstruction requirements associated with defect morphology and scale by adapting the interpolation of higher-level context before fusion with local lateral features. Together, the two components connect morphology-informed context encoding with spatial reconstruction within a compact parameter budget. The detector follows an ONNX-to-RKNN deployment workflow for the RK3566 edge board.

The main contributions of our work are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\arabic{enumi})}
  \item We design and implement a dedicated detection system for circular knitting machines, integrating image acquisition, camera transmission, RK3566 edge inference, and local machine-stop control on application-specific hardware, and demonstrate its deployment on a real production line.
  \item We develop morphology-informed feature modeling for circular-knitting defects, explicitly coupling the continuity of elongated flaws with the localized appearance of holes. MCE embeds extended-context modulation in a partial C2f branch while preserving its local feature paths to address these complementary representation requirements with small additional overhead.
  \item We design GIU to accommodate the different reconstruction requirements of elongated defects and holes of varying sizes. A compact spatial gate adapts a convex combination of two fixed interpolation paths, balancing response replication and neighborhood smoothing in the top-down context path before fusion with local lateral features.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper first presents the system overview, its four subsystems, and their hardware implementation, then describes \model, its two components, and the model deployment procedure. The experimental sections then define the data and evaluation protocol before presenting detector comparisons and component ablations.

\section{Defect Detection and Closed-Loop Control System}
\label{sec:system-design}
\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:system-overview}
We design and implement a dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system for circular knitting machines, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:interactions}. Our system integrates a power adapter, a global-shutter camera with LED illumination, a SerDes image-transmission link, a Hall sensor, a self-developed RK3566 edge board, and a cloud platform. These components are organized into four functional subsystems: power supply, image acquisition and transmission, network communication, and processor-based local computation and control. The power subsystem supplies the mainboard, which in turn powers and controls the LED illumination, and the image subsystem delivers fabric images to the processor over the SerDes link. The processor performs local defect detection and machine-stop control. The network subsystem uploads defect images and records to the cloud and delivers alert notifications to workers, while also supporting equipment monitoring and quality analysis.

The complete closed-loop workflow begins when a worker starts the circular knitting machine. The Hall sensor detects machine rotation and sends a signal to the processor, which switches on the camera and LED illumination and enters the detection state. When a fabric defect is detected, the processor first sends a wired stop command to the machine controller to interrupt further defect propagation. The system then uploads the defect image and associated record to the cloud, which pushes an alert notification to inform the worker that intervention is required. The worker inspects the reported defect, resolves the production fault, and restarts the machine; the rotation signal then reactivates inspection. This sequence connects production, rotation sensing, image acquisition, defect detection, machine stopping, defect reporting, worker notification, and corrective intervention into a complete production--inspection--control loop. The following subsections describe the four subsystem designs, followed by their hardware implementation.

\subsection{Power Supply Subsystem}
To accommodate different industrial supply voltages across countries and regions, we design a power adapter with a 100--500 V AC input range and a 24 V DC output rated at 3 A. The adapter first supplies this output to the mainboard. The mainboard then supplies the LED illumination. By adjusting the current delivered to the LEDs, the mainboard controls the illumination brightness to suit the image-acquisition conditions. This arrangement integrates LED power delivery and brightness control into the mainboard.

\subsection{Image Acquisition and Transmission Subsystem}
Rapid fabric motion requires imaging that preserves defect geometry and fine spatial detail. We therefore select a high-resolution global-shutter camera, which exposes all pixels simultaneously to avoid motion-induced distortion from sequential row exposure \cite{baslerglobalshutter}. The selected VEYE MV-MIPI-SC130M monochrome camera uses a SmartSens SC130GS-M global-shutter sensor at $1280\times1024$ resolution. The illumination supports short-exposure imaging of the moving fabric. At a camera-to-fabric distance of approximately 10 cm, the field of view is approximately $16\times12$ cm, with the 16 cm dimension aligned with the horizontal direction of fabric motion.

Factory machinery creates an electromagnetically noisy environment, and installation requires the camera and edge device to be separated by a cable run. We therefore use serializer/deserializer (SerDes) transmission over shielded coaxial cable to accommodate remote camera placement and the interference environment \cite{verma2017serdes}. Specifically, the THCV241A serializer and THCV242A deserializer bridge the camera-side and processor-side MIPI CSI-2 interfaces over a V-by-One HS link. The devices support a maximum serial line rate of 4.0 Gb/s per lane, corresponding to a rated effective rate of 3.2 Gb/s \cite{thineTHCV241A,thineTHCV242A}.

The camera is configured to transmit $1280\times1024$ RAW10 images at 30 frames/s. With $b=10$ bits per pixel, the active-image payload rate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_{\mathrm{img}} &= W H b f_{\mathrm{cam}} \\
&=1280\times1024\times10\times30 \\
&=393.216\ \mathrm{Mb/s},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:serdes-bandwidth}
\end{equation}
where $W$ and $H$ denote the image width and height, and $f_{\mathrm{cam}}$ is the camera acquisition rate. This corresponds to 49.152 MB/s, approximately 12.3\% of the rated single-lane effective capacity, leaving nominal bandwidth headroom for transport overhead. The calculation excludes packet and framing overhead and compares the active-image load with the device rating. The 30 frames/s acquisition rate is distinct from the 10 frames/s minimum detection-throughput requirement derived below. The image stream is recovered at the edge device before being passed to the processor subsystem.


\subsection{Network Communication Subsystem}
The network communication hardware is integrated on the mainboard and comprises a 4G module, a SIM-card slot, and an antenna connector for an external antenna. The SIM card provides subscriber identification and authentication for cellular network access. The antenna connector supports an external antenna, allowing flexible antenna placement according to the installation conditions.

The 4G module provides the data link between the edge device and the cloud platform. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:interactions}, MQTT carries device-state and defect-event messages between the device and cloud, while an HTTP interface gives operators access to monitoring information and defect records. The cloud supports device-state visualization, remote configuration, defect statistics, and production-quality analysis.

When the edge device detects a defect, it uploads the defect record and image to the cloud, which pushes a defect alert to the operator interface. The alert provides the defect category, occurrence time, and image for review and troubleshooting. These network functions complement the local display and wired stop command: defect inference and machine-stop decisions are executed by the processor subsystem without waiting for cloud communication.

\subsection{Processor Subsystem}
\label{sec:coverage}
The processor is selected according to the inspection rate required by fabric motion and the camera field of view. For a design diameter of 40 in ($D=1.016$ m) and a rotation period of $T=2$ s, the fabric travels approximately $\pi D=3.192$ m per revolution. With an effective field-of-view width of $w=0.16$ m along the direction of motion, the required frame rate is
\begin{equation}
f_{\min}=\frac{\pi D}{Tw}
=\frac{\pi\times1.016}{2\times0.16}
\approx9.97\ \mathrm{frames/s}.
\label{eq:coverage-rate}
\end{equation}
We set the effective detection-throughput requirement to 10 frames/s, corresponding to 20 processed frames per revolution. Persistent needle or yarn abnormalities cause defects to propagate during knitting, providing further opportunities for detection in subsequent inspection cycles.

For this throughput target, we select the Rockchip RK3566 to balance processing capability, camera connectivity, and hardware cost. It integrates a quad-core Cortex-A55 CPU, an NPU rated at 1 TOPS for INT8 operations, and camera input interfaces \cite{rockchiprk3566}. Table~\ref{tab:hardware-platforms} compares hardware specifications of the referenced platforms and our edge device. The 10 frames/s value is the system design requirement.

\section{Detection Algorithm}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{MCGI-YOLO Architecture}
\model is developed from YOLOv8n to incorporate the morphology priors of circular-knitting defects under the computational constraints of the RK3566 platform \cite{ultralytics2023,rockchipmodelzoo}. Elongated defects motivate spatially extended contextual encoding, while localized holes motivate retention of local feature evidence. The detector uses MCE in the backbone to combine these two forms of evidence. GIU adapts the reconstruction of higher-level context in the top-down feature pyramid according to local feature content before fusion with lateral branches. The original local bottlenecks, bottom-up aggregation path, and three-scale detection head are retained.

Fig.~\ref{fig:network} shows the architecture. MCE is embedded in the C2f stages that produce the stride-8 and stride-16 backbone features, denoted $F_3$ and $F_4$. Each MCE block modulates only a subset of the final hidden C2f branch, while retaining the preceding branches and the local convolutional operations. The stride-32 feature $F_5$ is produced by the unchanged final backbone stage and spatial pyramid pooling. At an input size of $640\times640$, $F_3$, $F_4$, and $F_5$ have spatial sizes of $80\times80$, $40\times40$, and $20\times20$, respectively.

In the top-down path, GIU replaces both nearest-neighbor upsampling operations. The feature fusion is expressed as
\begin{align}
M_4 &= \mathcal{C}_4\bigl([\mathcal{U}_g(F_5),F_4]\bigr), \label{eq:neck-p4}\\
M_3 &= \mathcal{C}_3\bigl([\mathcal{U}_g(M_4),F_3]\bigr), \label{eq:neck-p3}
\end{align}
where $[\cdot,\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation, $\mathcal{C}_3$ and $\mathcal{C}_4$ are the original neck C2f blocks, and $\mathcal{U}_g$ denotes GIU. The resulting features pass through the retained bottom-up path and detection head \cite{lin2017fpn,liu2018panet}. MCE therefore changes the context available to feature extraction and fusion, whereas GIU changes how the coarse feature values are interpolated before fusion.

\subsection{Morphology-Aware Context Encoding}
Elongated defects and localized holes impose different requirements on feature representation. The former require contextual evidence over an extended spatial support to describe continuity, while the latter require local evidence of texture disruption and defect boundaries. MCE implements this morphology-informed encoding by supplementing retained local C2f processing with extended contextual descriptors. The defect categories describe dominant fabric directions and allow small deviations from the image axes. In the implementation, row and column pooling provides compact spatial descriptors; this operation is related to the directional encoding principle of coordinate attention \cite{hou2021coordinate}. The descriptors are combined with depthwise filtering and residual modulation of a partial hidden branch to complement the local feature paths.

\emph{1) Partial-Channel Placement:}
For an input $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times W}$, the first C2f convolution produces two hidden branches with $c=\lfloor C_{\mathrm{out}}/2\rfloor$ channels each. Omitting the batch dimension, the original feature extraction is
\begin{align}
(Z_0,Z_1) &= \operatorname{Split}\bigl(\phi_1(X)\bigr), \label{eq:pac-split}\\
Z_{i+1} &= \mathcal{B}_i(Z_i),\quad i=1,\ldots,n, \label{eq:pac-bottlenecks}
\end{align}
where $\phi_1$ is the original $1\times1$ convolution with batch normalization and SiLU, and $\mathcal{B}_i$ is an unchanged bottleneck. MCE acts only on the final hidden feature $F=Z_{n+1}$. It is split into an active part $F_a\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times H\times W}$ and a bypass part $F_b\in\mathbb{R}^{(c-q)\times H\times W}$, with
\begin{equation}
q=\max\bigl(1,\lfloor\rho c\rfloor\bigr),\qquad \rho=0.25.
\label{eq:pac-active-channels}
\end{equation}
Thus, the one-quarter ratio refers to the final hidden branch, rather than the complete C2f input or output.

\emph{2) Spatial Context Aggregation:}
For active channel $a$, the row and column descriptors are
\begin{align}
R_h(a,h,1) &= \frac{1}{W}\sum_{w=1}^{W}F_a(a,h,w), \label{eq:pac-row}\\
R_w(a,1,w) &= \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}F_a(a,h,w). \label{eq:pac-column}
\end{align}
Here, $R_h\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times H\times1}$ aggregates the full width while retaining row positions, and $R_w\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times1\times W}$ aggregates the full height while retaining column positions. The descriptors are filtered along their retained axes and combined as
\begin{equation}
T=\operatorname{DW}_{3\times1}(R_h)
 +\operatorname{DW}_{1\times3}(R_w).
\label{eq:pac-context}
\end{equation}
The sum uses spatial broadcasting to obtain $T\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times H\times W}$. Both depthwise convolutions have a bias and preserve the descriptor dimensions through padding; no batch normalization or activation is inserted between these filters and the sum. The long-range support comes from the full-axis means, while the small kernels filter the resulting descriptors.

\emph{3) Residual Modulation and Feature Reuse:}
The context response is converted into a signed modulation map:
\begin{align}
A &= 2\operatorname{HS}(T)-1, \label{eq:pac-map}\\
F'_a &= F_a+\gamma\odot F_a\odot A, \label{eq:pac-modulation}
\end{align}
where $\operatorname{HS}(t)=\min(1,\max(0,(t+3)/6))$ is Hardsigmoid, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication with broadcasting, and $\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times1\times1}$ is a learnable per-channel scale. Although $A\in[-1,1]$, no amplitude constraint is imposed on $\gamma$. The unchanged bypass channels are concatenated with the modulated channels, and the C2f output is
\begin{equation}
Y=\phi_2\bigl([Z_0,\ldots,Z_n,[F'_a,F_b]]\bigr),
\label{eq:pac-output}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_2$ is the original final $1\times1$ convolution with batch normalization and SiLU. The earlier branches and all original bottlenecks are reused, so directional aggregation complements an existing local feature path rather than replacing it.

The scale $\gamma$ is initialized to zero. Consequently, a MCE block starts with the same forward mapping as its corresponding C2f block when their shared parameters are identical. During learning, $\gamma$ controls the introduction of the directional correction. The two depthwise filters and channel scale add $9q$ parameters in total: $6q$ filter weights, $2q$ biases, and $q$ scale parameters. Descriptor filtering costs $3q(H+W)$ multiply--accumulate operations; pooling, broadcasting, and modulation additionally process the $H\times W$ feature grid. Partial-channel placement reduces this overhead while keeping the local branches available for holes and fine structures.

\subsection{Gated Interpolation Upsampling}
Circular-knitting defects exhibit variations in morphology and scale, including horizontally and vertically elongated flaws and holes of different sizes. These variations place different demands on response continuity, local concentration, and boundary representation during feature reconstruction. Fixed interpolation applies the same replication or smoothing rule throughout the feature map, motivating a content-adaptive interpolation strategy for heterogeneous local structures.

The lateral $F_3$ and $F_4$ branches already provide local texture and localization cues. GIU adapts the reconstruction of higher-level context before concatenation with these lateral features (Fig.~\ref{fig:giu}). Nearest-neighbor interpolation replicates source responses, whereas bilinear interpolation combines neighboring responses using fixed geometric weights. A compact spatial gate, predicted from the coarse input features, adjusts the balance between these two behaviors according to local feature content. The reconstructed context and the retained lateral features then jointly participate in fusion. Gated combinations of transformed and bypass signals have established precedents in highway networks \cite{srivastava2015highway}; the present design applies complementary gating to two fixed interpolation paths.

\emph{1) Two Fixed Interpolation Paths:}
For $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, the two outputs are
\begin{align}
N &= \operatorname{Nearest}_{2}(X), \label{eq:giu-nearest}\\
B &= \operatorname{Bilinear}_{2}(X), \label{eq:giu-bilinear}
\end{align}
where $N,B\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times2H\times2W}$. Bilinear interpolation uses \texttt{align\_corners=False}. Neither path changes the channel dimension or introduces learned interpolation kernels.

\emph{2) Shared Spatial Gate:}
The gate is predicted at the input resolution:
\begin{equation}
G=\operatorname{Nearest}_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{HS}(W_g*X+b_g)\bigr),
\label{eq:giu-gate}
\end{equation}
where $*$ denotes convolution, $W_g\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C\times3\times3}$, and $b_g$ is a scalar bias. Padding of one preserves the low-resolution spatial dimensions, and no batch normalization is used. The gate $G\in[0,1]^{1\times2H\times2W}$ is broadcast across channels. Each low-resolution gate value is replicated over a $2\times2$ output block, avoiding an independent gate predictor at the higher resolution.

\emph{3) Gated Reconstruction:}
The two paths are combined as
\begin{align}
Y &= N+G\odot(B-N) \label{eq:giu-residual}\\
  &= (1-G)\odot N+G\odot B. \label{eq:giu-mixture}
\end{align}
For any channel and position, $Y$ lies between the corresponding values of $N$ and $B$. A gate near zero favors replication, whereas a gate near one favors bilinear interpolation. If the two paths agree, the gate has no effect. This constrains the learned correction to the difference between the two interpolation results; it does not synthesize an unrestricted residual feature. In particular, spatially constant input features remain constant after GIU, including at image boundaries under these interpolation settings.

The gate convolution is initialized with $W_g=0$ and $b_g=-2.7$, giving
\begin{equation}
G_{\mathrm{init}}=0.05,\qquad
Y_{\mathrm{init}}=0.95N+0.05B.
\label{eq:giu-initialization}
\end{equation}
The initial interpolation rule is therefore the same for a fixed input across random seeds, while the remainder of the detector still depends on initialization and training. Each GIU module adds $9C+1$ learned parameters, and its gate convolution costs $9CHW$ multiply--accumulate operations. The two interpolation paths, gate upsampling, and output mixing incur additional operations and memory traffic that must be included in device-level measurements.

MCE and GIU address the encoding and reconstruction stages of morphology-informed feature learning. MCE combines spatially extended context for elongated defects with retained local evidence for holes; GIU adapts the interpolation of higher-level context to local feature content, complementing the texture and localization cues retained in the lateral backbone features. Their design links the representation of defect continuity and locality with content-dependent spatial reconstruction within a small parameter budget. The individual and joint effects are evaluated through the four configurations defined in Section~\ref{sec:component-ablation}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation}
We report mean average precision at IoU $0.50$ (mAP50) and across ten IoU thresholds from $0.50$ to $0.95$ (mAP50--95), averaging over all defect categories \cite{lin2014coco}. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, intersection over union is $\operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=|B_p\cap B_g|/|B_p\cup B_g|$. Let $a_c(t)$ denote the average precision for class $c$ at IoU threshold $t$, and let $N_c$ denote the number of defect categories. The metrics are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
\operatorname{mAP}_{50:95} &= \frac{1}{10N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\sum_{t\in T}a_c(t),
\label{eq:ap}\\
\operatorname{mAP}_{50} &= \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}a_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
where $T=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$ and $N_c=3$ in this study. Both mAP metrics are reported as percentages.
mAP50--95 therefore evaluates localization over increasingly strict overlap requirements, whereas mAP50 uses a single, less restrictive threshold. Differences between reported mAP values are expressed in percentage points.

\subsection{Model Deployment and On-Device Performance}
\label{sec:deployment}
To deploy \model on the dedicated edge device, the PyTorch checkpoint selected using the validation set is first exported to ONNX with a fixed input size of $640\times640$. The ONNX graph is then converted into an INT8 RKNN model using RKNN-Toolkit2 \cite{rockchiptoolkit2,jacob2018quantization}. The converted model is loaded by an Android 13 application on the RK3566 and executed on its NPU. The application integrates the detector with defect visualization, local wired stop control, and cloud reporting. Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the installed system on the circular knitting machine.

For $640\times640$ inputs, the deployed INT8 model achieves a measured inference throughput of 22 FPS on the RK3566, equivalent to approximately 45.5 ms per frame. This throughput is 2.2 times the 10 FPS design target derived in Section~\ref{sec:coverage}, meeting the model-inference rate required for the specified machine speed and camera field of view. The reported FPS characterizes model inference; the complete acquisition-to-stop response also depends on image acquisition, preprocessing, postprocessing, and the machine's stopping dynamics.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents a dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system for circular knitting machines, together with the lightweight detector \model. The detector explicitly incorporates priors about the extended continuity of elongated defects and the localized appearance of holes. MCE combines spatially extended context with retained local C2f feature paths, while GIU adapts the reconstruction of higher-level context before fusion with local features to accommodate variations in defect morphology and scale. These complementary designs connect defect morphology modeling with spatial feature reconstruction under the computational constraints of an edge device.

The system integrates image acquisition, SerDes transmission, RK3566 local computation, rotation sensing, and wired machine-stop control. The deployed detector achieves an inference throughput of 22 frames/s on the RK3566. When a defect is detected, local control interrupts production and cloud communication supports defect reporting and worker notification. The component-ablation protocol evaluates MCE, GIU, and their combination against the same baseline; its final numerical findings will be incorporated with the corresponding detector comparison. Quantized-model accuracy, application response time, physical stopping delay, and false-stop frequency define the remaining system-level evaluation, together with generalization across fabric textures and production conditions.
