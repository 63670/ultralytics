\begin{abstract}
Circular knitting machines operate continuously, so defects caused by needle or yarn abnormalities may propagate over substantial fabric lengths before being discovered. To enable in-process defect detection and intervention, we develop a closed-loop defect detection system with edge inference and wired machine-stop control, and propose a lightweight detector termed \model. \model introduces defect morphology encoding (DME) to explicitly model horizontally elongated defects, vertically elongated defects, and localized hole-like defects, and content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR) to replace fixed feature-pyramid upsampling with content-adaptive reconstruction. On a production-line fabric-defect dataset, \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving the YOLOv8n baseline by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, with only 3.39 M parameters. The detector is deployed as an INT8 RKNN model on an RK3566 edge device. The resulting system couples real-time defect localization, automatic local stop control, operator review, and cloud-based quality analysis, providing a practical solution for reducing defect propagation during circular-knitting production.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Industrial defect detection, YOLO, deep learning, edge computing, Internet of Things.

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines are widely used in weft-knit fabric production and support high-rate, large-scale manufacturing \cite{cottonworksKnitBasics,woolmarkCircularKnitting}. The resulting fabrics are used in everyday garments such as T-shirts and sportswear \cite{cottonworksSingleDouble,woolmarkCircularKnitting}. The International Textile Manufacturers Federation reported global shipments of approximately 24,500 large circular knitting machines in 2025 \cite{itmf2025press}. Conventional quality checks are often performed after knitting is completed. Persistent needle or yarn abnormalities may therefore generate repeated defects along the fabric roll before detection, resulting in avoidable material loss and manufacturing costs \cite{takeuchi2018knitted,akter2022material,eeaTextiles}. This motivates in-process defect detection capable of identifying defects before they propagate over a substantial fabric length.

Single-stage YOLO detectors combine rapid inference with explicit bounding-box localization \cite{redmon2016yolo}, making them attractive for industrial defect detection. YOLO-based approaches have been applied to steel defects and large stamped parts \cite{gao2024srnyolo,li2024yoloxsteel,dong2024snf}. For fabrics, existing designs mainly pursue lightweight architectures for efficient inference \cite{kang2024ayolov7,yang2024yolov4tinys}, adaptive or deformable feature extraction for irregular defects \cite{luo2023deformableyolo,zhang2025dghr}, and improved multi-scale fusion or attention for small and weak defects \cite{yue2022tiny,yu2024fayolo,chen2025yolov8,wang2025srseyolo,gao2026yolov8,xu2025yolodll}. Together, these studies demonstrate the suitability of YOLO-based detectors for fabric defect detection. However, these improvements mainly emphasize detector efficiency or general feature enhancement; the characteristic spatial forms of fabric defects, including horizontally elongated, vertically elongated, and localized hole-like patterns, warrant more targeted consideration.

In circular-knitted fabrics, the three defect categories considered in this work exhibit distinct spatial morphologies: horizontally elongated defects, vertically elongated defects, and localized hole-like defects. Conventional feature extraction can learn such patterns implicitly, but does not explicitly separate directional and compact texture responses. In addition, fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling reconstructs higher-resolution pyramid features by replicating source responses without adapting to local texture content. Such reconstruction may inadequately preserve narrow defect boundaries or weak disruptions embedded in repetitive backgrounds.

Motivated by these characteristics, we develop \model by modifying feature encoding and feature reconstruction. Defect morphology encoding (DME) introduces horizontal, vertical, and local branches at selected backbone stages to strengthen the representation of the three characteristic defect morphologies. Content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR) predicts reassembly weights from local feature content and replaces fixed top-down feature-pyramid upsampling with location-adaptive neighborhood aggregation. Together, the two modules encode the spatial forms of defects in the backbone and adaptively reconstruct these features across pyramid scales.

To translate these detector improvements into production use, the model must operate in step with the machine and support local intervention. We deploy \model on an RK3566 edge device so that defect decisions do not depend on a cloud round trip \cite{shi2016edge}. Machine rotation detected by the Hall sensor activates local detection; a detected defect triggers wired stop control before the result is displayed for operator review. The IoT cloud receives defect and device records for monitoring and production-quality analysis, but does not participate in the stop decision.

The main contributions of our work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce DME to encode the characteristic morphologies of horizontal defects, vertical defects, and holes through horizontal, vertical, and local branches, providing a lightweight morphology-aware representation for circular-knitted defect detection.
  \item We incorporate CAFR into the feature pyramid to replace fixed upsampling with locally predicted reassembly weights, enabling content-adaptive reconstruction during multi-scale fusion.
  \item We implement a closed-loop production-line defect detection system integrating RK3566 edge inference, wired machine-stop control, operator review, and cloud-based monitoring, and demonstrate its deployment on a real circular-knitting production line.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Method}
\subsection{DMFR-YOLO Architecture}
\model is developed based on YOLOv8n, which is selected for its compatibility with RK3566 deployment, compact model size, and modular architecture \cite{ultralytics2023,rockchipmodelzoo}. The proposed modifications focus on defect morphology encoding in the backbone and feature reconstruction in the feature pyramid.

Fig.~\ref{fig:network} shows the overall architecture. DME is introduced after the main backbone stages to enhance the representation of different defect morphologies. The extracted multi-scale features are then passed to the neck for feature fusion \cite{lin2017fpn,liu2018panet}. Within the feature pyramid, CAFR replaces two nearest-neighbor upsampling operations with content-adaptive feature reassembly to better preserve defect boundaries and weak responses. The fused features are subsequently used by the multi-scale detection head to predict defect locations and categories.

\subsection{Defect Morphology Encoding}
The three defect categories considered in this work present different spatial characteristics. Horizontal and vertical defects usually extend along one dominant direction, whereas hole defects are more localized. To capture these differences, we introduce a defect morphology encoding (DME) module with three parallel branches, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dme}. The horizontal and vertical branches use $1\times7$ and $7\times1$ depthwise convolutions to enlarge the receptive field along the corresponding spatial directions, while the $3\times3$ branch captures localized defect structures. Depthwise convolution is adopted to model these spatial patterns with low computational overhead before channel-wise feature fusion \cite{howard2017mobilenets}.

For a C2f output $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, the three branch responses are
\begin{align}
F_h &= \operatorname{DW}_{1\times7}(X), \label{eq:dme-horizontal}\\
F_v &= \operatorname{DW}_{7\times1}(X), \label{eq:dme-vertical}\\
F_l &= \operatorname{DW}_{3\times3}(X). \label{eq:dme-local}
\end{align}
Here, $\operatorname{DW}$ denotes depthwise convolution followed by batch normalization and SiLU activation. All branches preserve the spatial dimensions and produce $C$ output channels. Their concatenation is fused as
\begin{equation}
F_d=\phi\left([F_h,F_v,F_l]\right),
\label{eq:dme-fusion}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation and $\phi$ is a $1\times1$ convolution mapping $3C$ channels to $C$, followed by batch normalization and SiLU. This learnable fusion combines the three types of spatial evidence rather than assigning a fixed branch to a particular defect class. A residual connection \cite{he2016resnet} gives
\begin{equation}
Y=X+F_d.
\label{eq:dme-residual}
\end{equation}

The residual connection combines the morphology-aware features with the incoming C2f output, allowing DME to enhance the backbone representation without discarding the features learned by the preceding layers. Its directional filtering is informed by asymmetric convolution designs \cite{ding2019acnet}.

\subsection{Content-Adaptive Feature Reassembly}
In the feature pyramid, nearest-neighbor upsampling enlarges feature maps by directly replicating feature values. This fixed operation does not adapt to the spatial structure of defects. To address this limitation, we introduce content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR), following the reassembly mechanism of CARAFE \cite{wang2019carafe}. CAFR predicts spatially varying kernels from the input features and uses them to aggregate local neighborhoods (Fig.~\ref{fig:cafr}), aiming to better preserve defect boundaries and weak responses during multi-scale fusion.

In \model, CAFR replaces the upsampling operations at both top-down fusion stages, with an upsampling factor of $s=2$ and a reassembly kernel size of $k=5$. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cafr}, CAFR consists of two parallel branches for kernel prediction and local feature extraction. Their outputs are subsequently combined through weighted aggregation to generate the upsampled features. The two branches and their subsequent feature reassembly are described as follows.

\emph{1) Reassembly Kernel Prediction:}
Given an input feature map $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, a $1\times1$ convolution first reduces its channel dimension:
\begin{equation}
X_c=E_{1\times1}(X).
\label{eq:cafr-compression}
\end{equation}
Here, $X_c\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times H\times W}$, where $D=\max(\lfloor C/4\rfloor,16)$ denotes the compressed channel count; $E_{1\times1}$ includes batch normalization and SiLU activation. A linear $3\times3$ convolution then predicts the unnormalized reassembly weights:
\begin{equation}
K'=E_{3\times3}(X_c).
\label{eq:cafr-logits}
\end{equation}
The resulting tensor is $K'\in\mathbb{R}^{s^2k^2\times H\times W}$. For $s=2$ and $k=5$, this corresponds to 100 weight channels at each input location. Pixel Shuffle rearranges the predicted weights onto the higher-resolution grid, followed by Softmax normalization:
\begin{equation}
K=\operatorname{Softmax}\!\left(\operatorname{PS}(K')\right).
\label{eq:cafr-kernel}
\end{equation}
Softmax is applied along the $k^2$ neighborhood dimension. The resulting tensor $K\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2\times sH\times sW}$ provides a normalized reassembly kernel for each output location.

\emph{2) Local Feature Extraction:}
In parallel, the patch branch extracts local neighborhoods from the input feature map using Unfold. The extracted patches are reshaped and upsampled using nearest-neighbor interpolation to match the spatial resolution of the predicted reassembly kernels:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P' &= \operatorname{Reshape}\!\left(\operatorname{Unfold}_{k\times k}(X)\right),\\
P &= \operatorname{Reshape}\!\left(\operatorname{Nearest}_{s}(P')\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cafr-patches}
\end{equation}
Here, $\operatorname{Unfold}_{k\times k}$ extracts a $k\times k$ neighborhood around each input position, and $\operatorname{Nearest}_{s}$ denotes nearest-neighbor upsampling with scale factor $s=2$. The first reshape produces $P'\in\mathbb{R}^{Ck^2\times H\times W}$; the final reshape gives the aligned patch tensor $P\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times k^2\times sH\times sW}$, where $k^2$ indexes the positions within each local neighborhood. Nearest-neighbor interpolation is used only for neighborhood alignment, not to generate the final output features.

\emph{3) Feature Reassembly:}
The predicted kernels and the aligned local features are combined through element-wise multiplication and summation along the neighborhood dimension:
\begin{equation}
Y=\sum_{u=1}^{k^2}K_u\odot P_u.
\label{eq:cafr-reassembly}
\end{equation}
Here, $K_u\in\mathbb{R}^{sH\times sW}$ and $P_u\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times sH\times sW}$ denote the weight map and corresponding local feature map for the $u$-th neighborhood position, respectively. The symbol $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, with the weights broadcast across feature channels, producing an output feature map $Y\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times sH\times sW}$.

By predicting location-specific reassembly weights, CAFR adaptively aggregates local features during upsampling, helping preserve defect-related information in multi-scale feature fusion.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Detection System}
\label{sec:deployment}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the installed system. After the operator starts the circular knitting machine, the Hall sensor detects rotation and signals the RK3566 edge device to switch on the LED light and begin detection from the camera video stream. When a defect is detected, the device sends a stop signal to the machine controller through a wired connection to interrupt further defect propagation. The defect image and detection result are displayed locally for operator review and troubleshooting. The operator resolves the problem and restarts the machine, completing the operational cycle.

The deployment path is PyTorch $\rightarrow$ ONNX $\rightarrow$ INT8 RKNN. The trained model is exported with a fixed $640\times640$ input and converted using RKNN-Toolkit2 \cite{rockchiptoolkit2}, with post-training INT8 quantization for efficient NPU execution \cite{jacob2018quantization}. An Android application executes the RKNN model on the RK3566 edge device.

The cloud platform receives defect records and device states for centralized monitoring, defect statistics, and production-quality analysis. Its interface supports device-state visualization, remote configuration, and review of uploaded defect images (Fig.~\ref{fig:cloud}). Detection and wired stop control remain local and do not depend on cloud communication.

A global-shutter industrial camera captures $1280\times1024$ frames approximately 10 cm from the fabric, with a field of view of approximately $16\times14$ cm. The project team retained defect-containing images and manually annotated axis-aligned bounding boxes. \subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation}
We report COCO average precision (AP) across ten IoU thresholds and AP at IoU $0.50$ (AP50) \cite{lin2014coco}. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, intersection over union is $\operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=|B_p\cap B_g|/|B_p\cup B_g|$. Let $\operatorname{AP}_c(t)$ denote the average precision for class $c$ at IoU threshold $t$, and let $N_c$ denote the number of defect categories. The metrics are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
\operatorname{AP} &= \frac{1}{10N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\sum_{t\in T}\operatorname{AP}_c(t),
\label{eq:ap}\\
\operatorname{AP}_{50} &= \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\operatorname{AP}_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
where $T=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$ and $N_c=3$ in this study. All AP values are reported as percentages.
AP therefore evaluates localization over increasingly strict overlap requirements, whereas AP50 uses a single, less restrictive threshold. Differences between reported AP values are expressed in percentage points.

All models are evaluated on the same held-out test set using their respective implementations. Since evaluation and post-processing settings differ across frameworks, the comparison with YOLOv8n and the component ablations serve as the primary controlled evaluations.

Parameter counts and approximate FLOPs are obtained from framework-native profiling at input shape $[1,3,640,640]$ (fused summaries for YOLO), with approximately two operations per multiply--accumulate.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Comparison With Other Detectors}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} presents the detection results on the test set. \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving the YOLOv8n baseline by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively. These improvements are achieved with only 0.38 M additional parameters and approximately 0.8 G additional FLOPs. The larger improvement in AP indicates better detection performance under stricter IoU thresholds.

Among the evaluated lightweight YOLO detectors, \model achieves the highest AP. Although RT-DETR-R18 attains a slightly higher AP of 67.7\%, it requires 20.09 M parameters and 60.72 G FLOPs, compared with 3.39 M parameters and 8.90 G FLOPs for \model. Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff} further illustrates the favorable balance between detection accuracy and model complexity achieved by \model.

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows predictions on seven selected test images, allowing qualitative comparison of missed defects, extra detections, and bounding-box alignment against repetitive fabric backgrounds.

\subsection{Component Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} evaluates the individual and combined effects of DME and CAFR on detection performance. Introducing DME increases AP from 59.3\% to 64.1\%, while CAFR alone improves AP to 60.7\%. When both modules are incorporated, \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, outperforming either individual modification. These results demonstrate the complementary effects of the two modules. DME enhances the representation of defect morphologies in the backbone, while CAFR adaptively reconstructs these features during multi-scale fusion. Their combination further improves the overall detection performance.

Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmaps} presents LayerCAM-based visualizations of YOLOv8n and \model generated using the same procedure. The examples illustrate differences in feature responses around defect regions, providing a qualitative comparison of the two models. Heat intensity is normalized per map and is not an absolute measure of detection accuracy.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents \model and a defect detection system for circular knitting machines. \model introduces defect morphology encoding (DME) to capture the spatial characteristics of horizontal defects, vertical defects, and holes. Content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR) improves feature reconstruction during multi-scale fusion. Experiments on the production line dataset show that \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, outperforming YOLOv8n by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, with 3.39 M parameters and 8.9 G FLOPs. The ablation results further demonstrate the complementary effects of DME and CAFR.

The proposed detector is deployed on an RK3566 edge device and integrated with machine monitoring and wired stop control. The system enables automatic defect detection and timely intervention without relying on cloud communication. Detection results are displayed locally for operators, while the cloud platform supports equipment monitoring and production quality analysis. By integrating defect detection with machine control and quality management, this work provides a complete and practical solution for fabric defect inspection in circular knitting production. Future work will explore the application of \model to different knitted fabrics with varying texture patterns and defect characteristics to evaluate its generalization across production conditions.
