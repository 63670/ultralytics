\begin{abstract}
Circular knitting machines operate continuously, so defects caused by needle or yarn abnormalities may propagate over substantial fabric lengths before being discovered. To enable in-process defect detection and intervention, we develop a closed-loop defect detection system with edge inference and wired machine-stop control, and propose a lightweight detector termed \model. \model introduces directional texture encoding (DTE) to explicitly model horizontal, vertical, and compact local disruptions of repetitive knitted texture, and local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) to replace fixed feature-pyramid upsampling with content-adaptive reconstruction. On a production-line fabric-defect dataset, \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving the YOLOv8n baseline by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, with only 3.39 M parameters. After INT8 deployment, the detector runs at 28 FPS on the RK3566 edge device. The resulting system couples real-time defect localization, automatic local stop control, operator review, and cloud-based quality analysis, providing a practical solution for reducing defect propagation during circular-knitting production.
\end{abstract}

Industrial defect detection, YOLO, deep learning, edge computing, Internet of Things.

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines are widely used in weft-knit fabric production and support high-rate, large-scale manufacturing \cite{cottonworksKnitBasics,woolmarkCircularKnitting}. The resulting fabrics are used in everyday garments such as T-shirts and sportswear \cite{cottonworksSingleDouble,woolmarkCircularKnitting}. The International Textile Manufacturers Federation reported global shipments of approximately 28,000 large circular knitting machines in 2024 \cite{itmf2024press}. Conventional quality checks are often performed after knitting is completed. Persistent needle or yarn abnormalities may therefore generate repeated defects along the fabric roll before detection, resulting in avoidable material loss and manufacturing costs \cite{takeuchi2018knitted,akter2022material,eeaTextiles}. This motivates in-process defect detection capable of identifying defects before they propagate over a substantial fabric length.

Single-stage YOLO detectors combine rapid inference with explicit bounding-box localization \cite{redmon2016yolo}, making them attractive for industrial defect detection. YOLO-based approaches have been applied to steel defects and large stamped parts \cite{gao2024srnyolo,li2024yoloxsteel,dong2024snf}. For fabrics, existing designs mainly pursue lightweight architectures for efficient inference \cite{kang2024ayolov7,yang2024yolov4tinys}, adaptive or deformable feature extraction for irregular defects \cite{luo2023deformableyolo,zhang2025dghr,zhou2025dcfe}, and improved multi-scale fusion or attention for small and weak defects \cite{yue2022tiny,yu2024fayolo,chen2025yolov8,wang2025srseyolo,gao2026yolov8,xu2025yolodll}. Together, these studies demonstrate the suitability of YOLO-based detectors for fabric defect detection. However, these improvements mainly emphasize detector efficiency or general feature enhancement; the repetitive texture structure characteristic of circular-knitted fabrics warrants more targeted consideration.

In circular-knitted fabrics, the three defect categories considered in this work---horizontal defects, vertical defects, and holes---typically manifest as horizontally extended, vertically extended, and compact local disruptions of the surrounding texture, respectively. Conventional feature extraction can learn such patterns implicitly, but does not explicitly separate directional and compact texture responses. In addition, fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling reconstructs higher-resolution pyramid features by replicating source responses without adapting to local texture content. Such reconstruction may inadequately preserve narrow defect boundaries or weak disruptions embedded in repetitive backgrounds.

Motivated by these characteristics, we develop \model by modifying feature encoding and feature reconstruction. Directional texture encoding (DTE) introduces horizontal, vertical, and local branches at selected backbone stages to strengthen the representation of the three characteristic defect morphologies. Local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) replaces fixed top-down feature-pyramid upsampling with location-adaptive neighborhood aggregation. Together, the two modules enhance texture representation in the backbone and feature reconstruction across pyramid scales.

To translate these detector improvements into production use, the model must operate in step with the machine and support local intervention. We deploy \model on an RK3566 edge device so that defect decisions do not depend on a cloud round trip \cite{shi2016edge}. Machine rotation detected by the Hall sensor activates local detection; a detected defect triggers wired stop control before the result is displayed for operator review. The IoT cloud receives defect and device records for monitoring and production-quality analysis, but does not participate in the stop decision.

The main contributions of our work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce DTE to encode the characteristic morphologies of horizontal defects, vertical defects, and holes through horizontal, vertical, and local texture branches, providing a lightweight texture-specific representation for circular-knitted defect detection.
  \item We develop an LTFR pyramid that replaces fixed upsampling with local texture-conditioned reassembly, enabling adaptive feature reconstruction during multi-scale fusion.
  \item We implement a closed-loop production-line defect detection system integrating RK3566 edge inference, wired machine-stop control, operator review, and cloud-based monitoring, and validate its deployment with production-line data and on-device measurements.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Method}
\subsection{TEFR-YOLO Architecture}
\model is instantiated on YOLOv8n, selected for its RK3566 deployment compatibility, compact scale, and modular structure \cite{ultralytics2023,rockchipmodelzoo}. The proposed modifications target backbone texture encoding and top-down feature reconstruction.

Fig.~\ref{fig:network} shows the overall architecture. DTE augments the output of each of the four backbone C2f stages at strides 4, 8, 16, and 32 with directional and local texture cues. The neck uses top-down and bottom-up multi-scale aggregation \cite{lin2017fpn,liu2018panet}. Within its top-down path, LTFR replaces the two nearest-neighbor upsampling operations: the first reconstructs stride-32 features at stride 16, and the second reconstructs the fused stride-16 features at stride 8. Each performs a single $2\times$ reconstruction before lateral fusion. The resulting three detection scales have spatial sizes $80\times80$, $40\times40$, and $20\times20$ for a $640\times640$ input.

\subsection{Directional Texture Encoding}
The three defect categories studied here exhibit characteristic spatial forms: horizontal defects typically extend along the image width, vertical defects along the image height, and holes appear as compact local discontinuities. DTE therefore uses horizontal, vertical, and local branches (Fig.~\ref{fig:dte}) to emphasize these characteristic forms of texture disruption. The directional branches provide elongated spatial support, while the local branch captures compact neighborhoods without favoring an orientation. Depthwise filtering separates spatial processing from subsequent channel mixing, following the efficiency principle of depthwise-separable networks \cite{howard2017mobilenets}.

For a C2f output $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, the three branch responses are
\begin{align}
F_h &= \operatorname{DW}_{1\times7}(X), \label{eq:dte-horizontal}\\
F_v &= \operatorname{DW}_{7\times1}(X), \label{eq:dte-vertical}\\
F_l &= \operatorname{DW}_{3\times3}(X). \label{eq:dte-local}
\end{align}
Here, $\operatorname{DW}$ denotes depthwise convolution followed by batch normalization and SiLU activation. Unit stride and symmetric padding preserve the spatial dimensions. The $1\times7$ and $7\times1$ kernels extend support along the horizontal and vertical image axes, respectively, while the $3\times3$ branch encodes compact neighborhoods. These directions describe image orientations rather than yarn systems. All branches produce $C$ channels. Their concatenation is fused as
\begin{equation}
F_d=\phi\left([F_h,F_v,F_l]\right),
\label{eq:dte-fusion}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation and $\phi$ is a $1\times1$ convolution mapping $3C$ channels to $C$, followed by batch normalization and SiLU. This learnable fusion mixes the three types of texture evidence rather than assigning a fixed branch to a particular defect class. A residual connection \cite{he2016resnet} gives
\begin{equation}
Y=X+F_d.
\label{eq:dte-residual}
\end{equation}

The identity path preserves the incoming C2f representation, while the residual branch learns a texture-dependent correction. DTE draws on asymmetric spatial filtering \cite{ding2019acnet}, combining orthogonal depthwise branches with compact local filtering and residual fusion after each selected backbone stage. This arrangement adds explicit texture encoding to the aggregated backbone features rather than repeating directional processing within individual bottlenecks.

\subsection{Local Texture-Guided Feature Reassembly}
Top-down fusion transfers semantic information from coarse maps to finer maps containing more spatial detail. With nearest-neighbor upsampling, this transfer repeats source features using fixed assignments, irrespective of whether a neighborhood contains regular texture or a defect boundary. LTFR instead predicts a normalized local reconstruction kernel for each output location, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ltfr}.

The reassembly formulation follows CARAFE \cite{wang2019carafe}. We use this mechanism at both top-down fusion stages with a compression ratio of four, scale factor $s=2$, and reassembly size $k=5$. Given $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, the kernel-prediction branch first compresses the channels to $C_r=\max(\lfloor C/4\rfloor,16)$:
\begin{equation}
X_c=E_{1\times1}(X).
\label{eq:ltfr-compression}
\end{equation}
The compressor $E_{1\times1}$ includes batch normalization and SiLU. A linear $3\times3$ convolution then predicts $s^2k^2=100$ kernel logits at each input location:
\begin{equation}
K'=E_{3\times3}(X_c).
\label{eq:ltfr-logits}
\end{equation}
Pixel shuffle rearranges these logits onto the enlarged grid,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{K}=\operatorname{PS}(K'),
\label{eq:ltfr-pixelshuffle}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2\times sH\times sW}$. Softmax along the $k^2$ neighborhood dimension produces location-specific weights:
\begin{equation}
K_{i,j}=\operatorname{softmax}(\widehat{K}_{i,j}).
\label{eq:ltfr-kernel}
\end{equation}
The normalization constraint is
\begin{equation}
\sum_{u=1}^{k^2}K_{i,j,u}=1,\qquad K_{i,j,u}\geq0.
\label{eq:ltfr-normalization}
\end{equation}
In parallel, $\operatorname{Unfold}_{k\times k}$ extracts source neighborhoods with unit stride and zero padding of $(k-1)/2$. For a zero-based output location $(i,j)$, the corresponding source neighborhood is
\begin{equation}
P_{i,j}=\operatorname{Unfold}_{k\times k}(X)_{\lfloor i/s\rfloor,\lfloor j/s\rfloor},
\label{eq:ltfr-patch}
\end{equation}
where $P_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times k^2}$. This alignment replicates source neighborhoods onto the enlarged grid without replacing the learned output-specific weights. The same spatial weights are applied to every feature channel:
\begin{equation}
Y_{c,i,j}=\sum_{u=1}^{k^2}K_{i,j,u}P_{c,i,j,u}.
\label{eq:ltfr-reassembly}
\end{equation}

The output has shape $C\times sH\times sW$. Equation~\eqref{eq:ltfr-reassembly} allows reconstruction weights to vary with local texture content, so defect boundaries and regular knitted regions can be aggregated differently. Channel compression limits kernel-prediction overhead. In \model, this CARAFE-based mechanism reconstructs the texture-encoded features at the two top-down pyramid stages before lateral fusion, adapting their spatial aggregation as information passes from stride 32 to stride 16 and then to stride 8.

DTE and LTFR play complementary roles: DTE enhances direction-sensitive texture representation in the backbone, while LTFR performs content-adaptive reconstruction of these features across pyramid scales.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Detection System}
\label{sec:deployment}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the installed system. After the operator starts the circular knitting machine, the Hall sensor detects rotation and signals the RK3566 edge device to switch on the LED light and begin detection from the camera video stream. When a defect is detected, the device sends a stop signal to the machine controller through a wired connection to interrupt further defect propagation. It then displays the defect image and detection result for on-site operator review and troubleshooting. The operator resolves the problem and restarts the machine, completing the operational cycle.

The deployment path is PyTorch $\rightarrow$ ONNX $\rightarrow$ INT8 RKNN. The trained model is exported with a fixed $640\times640$ input and converted using RKNN-Toolkit2 \cite{rockchiptoolkit2}, with post-training INT8 quantization for efficient NPU execution \cite{jacob2018quantization}. An Android application executes the RKNN model on the RK3566 edge device.

The cloud platform receives defect records and device states for centralized monitoring, defect statistics, and production-quality analysis. Its interface supports device-state visualization, remote configuration, and review of uploaded defect images (Fig.~\ref{fig:cloud}). Detection and wired stop control remain local and do not depend on cloud communication.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Dataset}
Images were collected during operation of a real circular-knitting production line using the arrangement in Section~\ref{sec:deployment}. A global-shutter industrial camera captures $1280\times1024$ frames at 30 FPS, approximately 10 cm from the fabric, with a field of view of approximately $16\times14$ cm. The project team retained defect-containing images and manually annotated axis-aligned bounding boxes. The engineering labels \emph{row}, \emph{col}, and \emph{hole} denote predominantly horizontal disruptions, vertical disruptions, and holes, respectively. These short labels are retained in detection displays, while their visual meanings are used in the analysis.

The dataset contains 403 images and 468 annotated instances. Its nonoverlapping training, validation, and test subsets contain 282, 40, and 81 images, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Validation data are used for checkpoint selection, and detection accuracy is reported on the held-out test set of 81 images and 91 instances.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation}
We report average precision across the ten IoU thresholds used in COCO evaluation \cite{lin2014coco}, together with AP at IoU $0.50$. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, intersection over union is $\operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=|B_p\cap B_g|/|B_p\cup B_g|$. At threshold $t$, a correct detection must have the correct class and match an otherwise unmatched ground-truth instance. Let $\operatorname{AP}_c(t)$ denote the area under the interpolated precision--recall curve for class $c$, expressed on $[0,1]$. For $N_c=3$ classes, the percentage-valued metrics are
\begin{align}
\operatorname{AP} &= \frac{100}{10N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{AP}_c(t), \notag\\
\operatorname{AP50} &= \frac{100}{N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\operatorname{AP}_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{T}=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$. 
AP therefore evaluates localization over increasingly strict overlap requirements, whereas AP50 uses a single, less restrictive threshold. Differences between percentage-valued AP scores are reported in percentage points.

All models are evaluated on the same held-out test images using their respective implementations and native evaluation and post-processing settings. Because evaluator details differ across frameworks, the direct YOLOv8n comparison and module ablations serve as the primary controlled comparisons, while cross-framework results provide broader references.

Model scale is reported as parameter counts and approximate FLOPs at input shape $[1,3,640,640]$. Counts follow framework-native profiling (fused summaries for YOLO), with approximately two operations per multiply--accumulate; unsupported operators may be omitted. On-device throughput is evaluated separately on RK3566.

\section{Results and Discussion}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} presents the test results. \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, compared with 59.3\% and 94.1\% for YOLOv8n. \subsection{Per-Class and Qualitative Results}
The class-wise AP values of \model are 61.7\%, 73.8\%, and 63.4\% for \emph{row}, \emph{col}, and \emph{hole}, respectively. The corresponding YOLOv8n values are 62.2\%, 59.6\%, and 56.2\%. The aggregate improvement therefore comes primarily from vertical defects (+14.2 points) and holes (+7.2 points), while horizontal-defect AP decreases slightly by 0.5 points. This result supports a more specific interpretation than uniform improvement across all morphologies. The vertical category contains only 12 test instances, so the magnitude of its gain should not be generalized beyond the evaluated sample without further data.

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} compares seven selected test images using common colors, line widths, and a display confidence threshold of 0.25. These examples allow visual comparison of missed detections, extra predictions, and box alignment against repetitive texture. They illustrate individual predictions rather than establish a population-level ranking; the quantitative comparison uses the full test set.

Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmaps} provides complementary, illustrative activation maps generated by the local LayerCAM-based implementation \cite{jiang2021layercam}. Positive class-score gradients weight the feature maps entering the three detection scales. Maps are spatially normalized for each candidate and scale, combined by a pixelwise maximum, and overlaid on the original image. The same procedure is used for both models, considering up to 16 highest-scoring candidates above 0.25, with a highest-score fallback if none exceed the threshold. The selected rows originate from the validation, training, and test subsets, respectively, and are not treated as additional held-out evidence. Per-map normalization and candidate selection also mean that heat intensity is not directly comparable as an absolute measure of model quality.

\subsection{Edge Deployment Evaluation}
\label{sec:edge-evaluation}
The INT8 \model deployed on the RK3566 edge device achieves 28 FPS. Together with Hall-triggered acquisition and wired machine-stop control, this deployment supports local defect detection and intervention without a cloud round trip. Defect images are displayed for on-site operator review and troubleshooting, while defect records and device states are uploaded to the cloud for equipment monitoring and production-quality analysis.

The current evaluation uses a small, imbalanced set of defect-containing images. Broader production trials are needed to assess transfer across materials and operating conditions, false stops during defect-free operation, and detection accuracy after INT8 conversion.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented \model and a production-line defect detection system for circular knitting machines. \model couples directional texture encoding with content-adaptive feature reconstruction for knitted-fabric defect detection. DTE models horizontal, vertical, and compact local texture disruptions in the backbone, while LTFR replaces fixed upsampling with CARAFE-based local reassembly in the top-down feature pyramid. On the production-line test set, \model improves the YOLOv8n baseline from 59.3\% to 66.3\% AP and from 94.1\% to 96.9\% AP50, with 3.39 M parameters and approximately 8.9 G FLOPs. These results, together with the component ablation, support the complementary roles of texture encoding and feature reassembly in improving overall localization performance while retaining a compact model scale.

The detector is deployed on an RK3566 edge device and reaches 28 FPS. Combined with Hall-sensor-based machine-state detection and wired stop control, the system performs local defect detection and intervention without depending on cloud communication. Detection results are displayed for on-site operator review and troubleshooting, while defect records and device states are uploaded to the cloud for equipment monitoring and production-quality analysis. This deployment connects the detector to local machine control, supporting not only offline defect detection but also in-process defect containment during circular-knitting production. Future work will focus on broader production conditions, long-term false-stop and missed-detection behavior, quantized-model accuracy, and finer-grained analysis of the proposed modules.
