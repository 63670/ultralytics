\begin{abstract}
Circular knitting machines are widely used in continuous fabric production. Needle or yarn abnormalities can cause repeated defects along the fabric before they are detected, resulting in material waste and economic losses. To enable in-process defect detection and intervention, we develop a closed-loop defect detection system with edge inference and wired machine-stop control, and propose a lightweight detector termed \model. \model introduces defect morphology encoding (DME) into the backbone to explicitly model horizontally elongated defects, vertically elongated defects, and localized hole-like defects. In the feature pyramid, content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR) replaces fixed upsampling with content-adaptive reconstruction, improving the preservation of defect boundaries and weak responses during multi-scale feature fusion. Evaluated on a self-collected fabric defect dataset from a real-world circular knitting machine production line, \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving the YOLOv8n baseline by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, with only 3.39 M parameters. The detector is deployed as an INT8 RKNN model on an RK3588 edge device. The resulting system couples real-time defect detection, automatic local stop control, operator review, and cloud-based quality analysis, providing a practical solution for reducing defect propagation during circular-knitting production.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines are important production infrastructure for high-volume weft-knit fabrics used in T-shirts and sportswear \cite{cottonworksKnitBasics,woolmarkCircularKnitting,cottonworksSingleDouble}. The International Textile Manufacturers Federation reported approximately 24,500 global shipments of large circular knitting machines in 2025 \cite{itmf2025press}. Globally, approximately 92 million tonnes of textile waste are generated annually, highlighting the importance of reducing avoidable material losses \cite{unep2025textilewaste}. Conventional quality checks are often performed after knitting is completed. A persistent needle or yarn abnormality can therefore generate repeated defects along a fabric roll before discovery, accumulating substantial fabric waste and economic losses \cite{takeuchi2018knitted,akter2022material,xiang2023hooknet}. Real-time detection during knitting is needed to identify these defects and enable intervention before further defective fabric is produced.

Recent studies have explored edge computing for fabric defect detection. Song et al. deploy EfficientDet-D0 on an NVIDIA Jetson TX2 for fabric defect detection \cite{song2021edgefabric}, while Machado et al. deploy YOLO11n on a Jetson Orin Nano for textile defect detection on a fabric rolling line \cite{machado2025edgefabric}. Yan et al. deploy an improved YOLOv5s model on a Huawei Ascend 310-based Atlas platform for warp-knitted lace defect detection \cite{yan2026warplace}. Beyond textiles, Wang et al. deploy an improved YOLOv5s model on a Jetson Xavier NX for rail surface defect detection \cite{wang2024rail}. Li et al. deploy LiteYOLO-ID on a Jetson TX2 NX for insulator defect detection \cite{li2024liteyolo}, and Zhao et al. implement KAM-Net on a Xilinx ZCU102 FPGA for glass defect segmentation \cite{zhao2025kamnet}. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:hardware-platforms}, these studies mainly employ general-purpose edge-computing platforms or FPGA development boards. Such platforms support a broad range of applications rather than the specific requirements of circular knitting machines. Their broad functionality and the additional hardware needed for application-specific integration can increase system cost, making large-scale industrial deployment more challenging. This motivates a dedicated edge device that integrates image acquisition, defect detection, and machine-stop control for circular knitting production.

At the algorithm level, YOLO-based detectors provide efficient inference and explicit defect localization \cite{redmon2016yolo}. Recent studies have incorporated YOLOv3 into an optical detection system for LCD module defects \cite{zhu2022lcdaoi}, combined template assistance and attention with YOLOv7 for paper defects \cite{li2024paperyolo}, and introduced feature prefusion and mask guidance into a Scaled-YOLOv4-style detector for camera-decoration surface defects \cite{wang2024fmn}. Lightweight convolution and structural reparameterization have further supported insulator and rail defect detection on embedded platforms \cite{li2024liteyolo,wang2024rail}. These works demonstrate the practicality of the YOLO route and improve defect representation or inference efficiency. For circular-knitted fabrics, our focus is to retain this efficiency while explicitly representing the characteristic shapes of defects in repetitive backgrounds.

The defects considered in this work appear as horizontally elongated defects, vertically elongated defects, and localized holes. Standard convolutional feature extraction can learn these patterns implicitly, but it does not explicitly separate their spatial responses. In addition, the nearest-neighbor upsampling used in conventional feature pyramids reconstructs higher-resolution features through fixed replication rather than local content-dependent aggregation. Preserving thin defect boundaries and weak local responses under a limited computational budget therefore motivates morphology-specific feature encoding and adaptive reconstruction during multi-scale fusion.

Based on these hardware and algorithm considerations, we design and implement a dedicated defect detection system for circular knitting machines and propose a lightweight detector, \model. The system integrates an industrial camera with LED illumination, a Hall sensor, a coaxial video link, and a self-developed RK3588 edge board. Machine rotation activates detection, and a detected defect triggers a local wired stop command and displays the result for operator review. The cloud receives defect and device records for monitoring and production-quality analysis without participating in the stop decision \cite{shi2016edge}. Within the detector, defect morphology encoding (DME) introduces horizontal, vertical, and local branches into the backbone, while content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR) replaces fixed top-down upsampling with locally predicted neighborhood aggregation. The hardware integration and compact detector jointly support detection and intervention during knitting.

The main contributions of our work are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\arabic{enumi})}
  \item We design and implement a dedicated detection system for circular knitting machines, integrating image acquisition, camera transmission, RK3588 edge inference, and local machine-stop control on application-specific hardware, and demonstrate its deployment on a real production line.
  \item We introduce DME to encode the characteristic morphologies of horizontal defects, vertical defects, and holes through horizontal, vertical, and local branches, providing a lightweight morphology-aware representation for circular-knitted defect detection.
  \item We incorporate CAFR into the feature pyramid to replace fixed upsampling with locally predicted reassembly weights, enabling content-adaptive reconstruction during multi-scale fusion.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hardware and Detection System}
\label{sec:system-design}
\subsection{Hardware Configuration}
The system combines image acquisition, machine-state sensing, edge computation, and a wired connection to the knitting-machine controller. Fig.~\ref{fig:hardware} shows the camera assembly and our self-developed RK3588 edge-device hardware. The industrial camera and LED light are integrated in the front assembly (A). We use a VEYE MV-MIPI-SC130M monochrome camera with a SmartSens SC130GS-M global-shutter sensor and a $1280\times1024$ imaging resolution. A camera-side interface board (B) provides a serializer and a coaxial connector, while the RK3588 edge board (C) includes a deserializer for the camera connection. This arrangement connects the camera mounted near the fabric to the edge device responsible for detection and machine control.

The RK3588 edge board integrates flash memory, power management, and input/output interfaces, together with a 4G module, SIM-card slot, and antenna connector for cloud communication. The Hall sensor provides the machine-rotation signal, and a wired connection to the machine controller carries the stop signal. Image processing and the stop decision remain on the edge device; cloud communication is used for device monitoring and defect records rather than for latency-critical control.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Detection Workflow}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the installation of our defect detection system on a circular knitting machine production line. After the operator starts the circular knitting machine, the Hall sensor detects rotation and signals the RK3588 edge device to switch on the LED light and begin detection from the camera video stream. When a defect is detected, the device sends a stop signal to the machine controller through a wired connection to interrupt further defect propagation. The defect image and detection result are displayed locally for operator review and troubleshooting. The operator resolves the problem and restarts the machine, completing the operational cycle.

\subsection{Edge Deployment and Cloud Platform}
\label{sec:deployment}
The detector described in Section~\ref{sec:method} is deployed locally on the RK3588 edge device. To enable on-device inference, the trained model is converted from PyTorch to ONNX and then to an INT8 RKNN model. The trained model is exported with a fixed $640\times640$ input and converted using RKNN-Toolkit2 \cite{rockchiptoolkit2}, with post-training INT8 quantization for efficient NPU execution \cite{jacob2018quantization}. An Android application executes the RKNN model on the RK3588 edge device.

The cloud platform receives defect records and device states for centralized monitoring, defect statistics, and production-quality analysis. Its interface supports device-state visualization, remote configuration, and review of uploaded defect images.

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{DMFR-YOLO Architecture}
\model is developed based on YOLOv8n, which is selected for its compatibility with RK3588 deployment, compact model size, and modular architecture \cite{ultralytics2023,rockchipmodelzoo}. The proposed modifications focus on defect morphology encoding in the backbone and feature reconstruction in the feature pyramid.

Fig.~\ref{fig:network} shows the overall architecture. DME is introduced after the main backbone stages to enhance the representation of different defect morphologies. The extracted multi-scale features are then passed to the neck for feature fusion \cite{lin2017fpn,liu2018panet}. Within the feature pyramid, CAFR replaces two nearest-neighbor upsampling operations with content-adaptive feature reassembly to better preserve defect boundaries and weak responses. The fused features are subsequently used by the multi-scale detection head to predict defect locations and categories.

\centering

\caption{Architecture of \model with an enlarged view of DME. DME follows the backbone C2f stages, and CAFR replaces the two top-down upsampling operations. The neck fusion blocks, bottom-up path, and three-scale detection head are retained from YOLOv8n.}
\label{fig:network}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Defect Morphology Encoding}
The three defect categories considered in this work present different spatial characteristics. Horizontal and vertical defects usually extend along one dominant direction, whereas hole defects are more localized. To capture these differences, we introduce a defect morphology encoding (DME) module with three parallel branches, as shown in the enlarged view in Fig.~\ref{fig:network}. The horizontal and vertical branches use $1\times7$ and $7\times1$ depthwise convolutions to enlarge the receptive field along the corresponding spatial directions, while the $3\times3$ branch captures localized defect structures. Depthwise convolution is adopted to model these spatial patterns with low computational overhead before channel-wise feature fusion \cite{howard2017mobilenets}.

For a C2f output $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, the three branch responses are
\begin{align}
F_h &= \operatorname{DW}_{1\times7}(X), \label{eq:dme-horizontal}\\
F_v &= \operatorname{DW}_{7\times1}(X), \label{eq:dme-vertical}\\
F_l &= \operatorname{DW}_{3\times3}(X). \label{eq:dme-local}
\end{align}
Here, $\operatorname{DW}$ denotes depthwise convolution followed by batch normalization and SiLU activation. All branches preserve the spatial dimensions and produce $C$ output channels. Their concatenation is fused as
\begin{equation}
F_d=\phi\left([F_h,F_v,F_l]\right),
\label{eq:dme-fusion}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation and $\phi$ is a $1\times1$ convolution mapping $3C$ channels to $C$, followed by batch normalization and SiLU. This learnable fusion combines the three types of spatial evidence rather than assigning a fixed branch to a particular defect class. A residual connection \cite{he2016resnet} gives
\begin{equation}
Y=X+F_d.
\label{eq:dme-residual}
\end{equation}

The residual connection combines the morphology-aware features with the incoming C2f output, allowing DME to enhance the backbone representation without discarding the features learned by the preceding layers. The directional branches are based on asymmetric convolution designs\cite{ding2019acnet}.

\subsection{Content-Adaptive Feature Reassembly}
In the feature pyramid, nearest-neighbor upsampling enlarges feature maps by directly replicating feature values. This fixed operation does not adapt to the spatial structure of defects. To address this limitation, we introduce content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR), following the reassembly mechanism of Content-Aware ReAssembly of FEatures(CARAFE) \cite{wang2019carafe}. CAFR predicts spatially varying kernels from the input features and uses them to aggregate local neighborhoods (Fig.~\ref{fig:cafr}), aiming to better preserve defect boundaries and weak responses during multi-scale fusion.

In \model, CAFR replaces the upsampling operations at both top-down fusion stages, with an upsampling factor of $s=2$ and a reassembly kernel size of $k=5$. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cafr}, CAFR consists of two parallel branches for kernel prediction and local feature extraction. Their outputs are subsequently combined through weighted aggregation to generate the upsampled features. The two branches and their subsequent feature reassembly are described as follows:

\emph{1) Reassembly Kernel Prediction:}
Given an input feature map $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, a $1\times1$ convolution first reduces its channel dimension:
\begin{equation}
X_c=E_{1\times1}(X).
\label{eq:cafr-compression}
\end{equation}
Here, $X_c\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times H\times W}$, where $D=\max(\lfloor C/4\rfloor,16)$ denotes the compressed channel count; $E_{1\times1}$ includes batch normalization and SiLU activation. A linear $3\times3$ convolution then predicts the unnormalized reassembly weights:
\begin{equation}
K'=E_{3\times3}(X_c).
\label{eq:cafr-logits}
\end{equation}
The resulting tensor is $K'\in\mathbb{R}^{s^2k^2\times H\times W}$. For $s=2$ and $k=5$, this corresponds to 100 weight channels at each input location. Pixel Shuffle rearranges the predicted weights onto the higher-resolution grid, followed by Softmax normalization:
\begin{equation}
K=\operatorname{Softmax}\!\left(\operatorname{PS}(K')\right).
\label{eq:cafr-kernel}
\end{equation}
Softmax is applied along the $k^2$ neighborhood dimension. The resulting tensor $K\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2\times sH\times sW}$ provides a normalized reassembly kernel for each output location.

\emph{2) Local Feature Extraction:}
In parallel, the patch branch extracts local neighborhoods from the input feature map using Unfold. The extracted patches are reshaped and upsampled using nearest-neighbor interpolation to match the spatial resolution of the predicted reassembly kernels:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P' &= \operatorname{Reshape}\!\left(\operatorname{Unfold}_{k\times k}(X)\right),\\
P &= \operatorname{Reshape}\!\left(\operatorname{Nearest}_{s}(P')\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cafr-patches}
\end{equation}
Here, $\operatorname{Unfold}_{k\times k}$ extracts a $k\times k$ neighborhood around each input position, and $\operatorname{Nearest}_{s}$ denotes nearest-neighbor upsampling with scale factor $s=2$. The first reshape produces $P'\in\mathbb{R}^{Ck^2\times H\times W}$; the final reshape gives the aligned patch tensor $P\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times k^2\times sH\times sW}$, where $k^2$ indexes the positions within each local neighborhood. Nearest-neighbor interpolation is used only for neighborhood alignment, not to generate the final output features.

\emph{3) Feature Reassembly:}
The predicted kernels and the aligned local features are combined through element-wise multiplication and summation along the neighborhood dimension:
\begin{equation}
Y=\sum_{u=1}^{k^2}K_u\odot P_u.
\label{eq:cafr-reassembly}
\end{equation}
Here, $K_u\in\mathbb{R}^{sH\times sW}$ and $P_u\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times sH\times sW}$ denote the weight map and corresponding local feature map for the $u$-th neighborhood position, respectively. The symbol $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, with the weights broadcast across feature channels, producing an output feature map $Y\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times sH\times sW}$.

By predicting location-specific reassembly weights, CAFR adaptively aggregates local features during upsampling, helping preserve defect-related information in multi-scale feature fusion.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Dataset}
Images were collected during operation of a real circular-knitting production line. A global-shutter industrial camera captures $1280\times1024$ frames approximately 10 cm from the fabric, with a field of view of approximately $16\times14$ cm. We selected images with visible defects and manually annotated their bounding boxes. The dataset includes three defect categories: horizontal defects (\emph{row}), vertical defects (\emph{col}), and holes (\emph{hole}).

The dataset contains 403 images with 468 annotated defect instances, divided into training, validation, and test sets of 282, 40, and 81 images, respectively, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:data}. The validation set is used for checkpoint selection, while performance is reported on the test set containing 91 labeled defects.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation}
We report COCO average precision (AP) across ten IoU thresholds and AP at IoU $0.50$ (AP50) \cite{lin2014coco}. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, intersection over union is $\operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=|B_p\cap B_g|/|B_p\cup B_g|$. Let $\operatorname{AP}_c(t)$ denote the average precision for class $c$ at IoU threshold $t$, and let $N_c$ denote the number of defect categories. The metrics are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
\operatorname{AP} &= \frac{1}{10N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\sum_{t\in T}\operatorname{AP}_c(t),
\label{eq:ap}\\
\operatorname{AP}_{50} &= \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\operatorname{AP}_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
where $T=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$ and $N_c=3$ in this study. All AP values are reported as percentages.
AP therefore evaluates localization over increasingly strict overlap requirements, whereas AP50 uses a single, less restrictive threshold. Differences between reported AP values are expressed in percentage points.

Model parameters and FLOPs are measured using the respective frameworks with an input resolution of 640 × 640.

\subsection{Comparison With Other Detectors}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} presents the detection results on the test set. \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving the YOLOv8n baseline by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively. These improvements are achieved with only 0.38 M additional parameters and approximately 0.8 G additional FLOPs. The larger improvement in AP indicates better detection performance under stricter IoU thresholds.

Among the evaluated lightweight YOLO detectors, \model achieves the highest AP. Although RT-DETR-R18 attains a slightly higher AP of 67.7\%, it requires 20.09 M parameters and 60.72 G FLOPs, compared with 3.39 M parameters and 8.90 G FLOPs for \model.

\subsection{Component Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} evaluates the individual and combined effects of DME and CAFR on detection performance. Introducing DME increases AP from 59.3\% to 64.1\%, while CAFR alone improves AP to 60.7\%. When both modules are incorporated, \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, outperforming either individual modification. These results demonstrate the complementary effects of the two modules. DME enhances the representation of defect morphologies in the backbone, while CAFR adaptively reconstructs these features during multi-scale fusion. Their combination further improves the overall detection performance.

Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmaps} compares the feature visualizations of YOLOv8n and \model. Compared with YOLOv8n, DMFR-YOLO exhibits more concentrated feature responses around elongated defects and holes, with activation regions more closely aligned with the defect shapes. These observations suggest that DMFR-YOLO provides a more focused representation of defect regions.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents \model and a defect detection system for circular knitting machines. \model introduces defect morphology encoding (DME) to capture the spatial characteristics of horizontal defects, vertical defects, and holes. Content-adaptive feature reassembly (CAFR) improves feature reconstruction during multi-scale fusion. Experiments on the production line dataset show that \model achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, outperforming YOLOv8n by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, with 3.39 M parameters and 8.9 G FLOPs. The ablation results further demonstrate the complementary effects of DME and CAFR.

The proposed detector is deployed on an RK3588 edge device and integrated with machine monitoring and wired stop control. The system enables automatic defect detection and timely intervention without relying on cloud communication. Detection results are displayed locally for operators, while the cloud platform supports equipment monitoring and production quality analysis. By integrating defect detection with machine control and quality management, this work provides a complete and practical solution for fabric defect detection in circular knitting production. Future work will explore the application of \model to different knitted fabrics with varying texture patterns and defect characteristics to evaluate its generalization across production conditions.
