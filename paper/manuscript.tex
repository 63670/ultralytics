\begin{abstract}
Circular knitting machines are foundational production infrastructure in the textile industry. A fabric defect arising during production can propagate during continuous knitting and cause substantial fabric loss if it is not detected and handled promptly. To address this issue, we design and implement a cloud-edge collaborative inspection system integrating a Hall sensor, adjustable supplementary lighting, an industrial high-definition camera, an RK3566 edge terminal, a textile-specific detector, and an IoT cloud platform. When the Hall sensor indicates that the machine is operating, the edge terminal activates the camera and supplementary light. Once a defect is detected, it stops the machine through the local wired control path and reports the defect to the cloud platform for defect statistics and process improvement. The proposed TFRA-YOLO starts from YOLOv8n and combines directional texture encoding (DTE) with local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) to retain directional knitted-texture cues and adaptively reconstruct the feature pyramid. On images collected from a real textile production line, TFRA-YOLO achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving YOLOv8n by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively. The proposed system provides a practical route from delayed post-production inspection toward traceable, in-process defect containment for circular knitting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines are foundational production infrastructure in the textile industry: they continuously manufacture the knitted fabrics used in T-shirts, sportswear, and other daily garments. The latest International Textile Manufacturers Federation (ITMF) statistics report 28,000 global shipments of large circular knitting machines in 2024 \cite{itmf2024itmss}. Conventional inspection is commonly performed after production. Consequently, a defect arising early in production may continue propagating as the machine operates, leading to substantial fabric waste before it is discovered. An effective inspection system must therefore detect and localize defects during machine operation with sufficiently low latency to enable intervention before defects propagate into substantial fabric waste.

Single-stage YOLO detectors are particularly attractive for this setting because they jointly provide real-time inference and explicit defect localization. YOLO-based detectors have been adopted for industrial surface inspection of steel strips, steel surfaces, and large stamped parts \cite{gao2024srnyolo,li2024yoloxsteel,dong2024snf}. In fabric-defect detection, existing studies have adapted YOLO through lightweight or tiny-model designs \cite{kang2024ayolov7,yang2024yolov4tinys,chen2025lwfdd}, deformable-convolution and feature-pyramid refinements \cite{luo2023deformableyolo,zhang2025dghr}, and multi-scale representation refinements for weak or small defects \cite{yue2022tiny,yu2024fayolo,chen2025yolov8,zhou2025dcfe,gao2026yolov8,xu2025yolodll,wang2025srseyolo}. These studies establish the suitability of the YOLO route for bounding-box localization under industrial latency and resource constraints. However, most existing modifications primarily strengthen generic feature representations rather than explicitly exploiting the directional texture structure of circular-knitted fabrics. YOLOv8n is adopted as the baseline because, on the official RK3566 benchmark, it achieves a higher inference frame rate than YOLO11n while retaining a more modern and structurally extensible architecture than YOLOv5n, making it suitable for both edge deployment and module-level modification \cite{rockchipmodelzoo}.

Two texture-specific limitations therefore remain when a default YOLOv8n is applied to circular-knitted fabric. The dataset contains \emph{row}, \emph{col}, and \emph{hole} defects, which typically appear as horizontal, vertical, and compact local disruptions of the repetitive knitted texture, respectively. Standard C2f processing can learn local defect patterns, but it does not explicitly introduce an orientation-aware inductive bias for separating these forms of texture disruption. Second, nearest-neighbor upsampling in the top-down feature pyramid reconstructs higher-resolution features with a content-independent copying rule rather than adapting reconstruction weights to local texture structure. Such content-independent reconstruction may blur or duplicate weak defect responses when thin defect boundaries are embedded in repetitive texture. These observations highlight the need to incorporate textile-specific structure beyond generic feature enhancement.

Based on this motivation, \model introduces texture-specific inductive biases at both feature encoding and feature reconstruction stages by adapting two key stages of the YOLOv8n detection pipeline. To address the first limitation, we introduce a directional texture encoding (DTE) module after selected C2f outputs. DTE separately models horizontal, vertical, and compact local texture evidence, while residual fusion retains the baseline representation and augments it with directional texture cues. To address the second limitation, we introduce a local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) pyramid that replaces the two fixed nearest-neighbor top-down upsampling operations with location-specific feature reassembly. \model retains the lightweight YOLOv8n framework while adding texture-aware representation at backbone encoding and content-adaptive reconstruction in the top-down feature pyramid.

Beyond detector design, practical deployment requires inference to be synchronized with the circular knitting machine and to support low-latency local intervention. We therefore integrate \model into a cloud-edge inspection system comprising rotation-synchronized image acquisition, RK3566-based edge inference, local wired stop control, and a cloud digital-twin platform. The Hall sensor provides machine-rotation information used to synchronize the inspection cycle. Once a defect is detected, the edge terminal immediately triggers the local wired stop-control path, after which the detection result is displayed for on-site operator inspection and fault diagnosis. Defect records and device states are synchronized to the cloud platform for status visualization, defect statistics, and process improvement, while the latency-critical stop-control loop remains entirely local and does not depend on cloud communication.

The main contributions of our work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce a directional texture encoding (DTE) module that explicitly decomposes horizontal, vertical, and compact local texture cues and fuses them with the baseline C2f representation, providing a lightweight texture-specific inductive bias for circular-knitted defect detection.
  \item We develop a local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) pyramid that replaces fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling with location-adaptive reconstruction, improving the preservation of weak defect structures during top-down multi-scale fusion.
  \item We implement a cloud-edge inspection prototype for circular knitting machines that integrates rotation-synchronized sensing, RK3566 edge inference, local wired stop control, and cloud-based digital-twin monitoring. The design keeps the latency-critical intervention loop local while supporting centralized defect statistics and device supervision.
\end{itemize}

\section{Closed-Loop Inspection Workflow}
\subsection{Production Workflow}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the deployed circular knitting machine and the installed start button, Hall sensor, industrial camera, and RK3566 edge device. The workflow begins when an operator presses the start button and the machine starts rotating. The Hall sensor detects this rotation and sends the signal to the RK3566 device, which activates the supplementary light, acquires the camera video stream, and enters the inspection state. The edge device performs detection locally. Once a defect is detected, the RK3566 sends a stop command through its wired connection to the machine control system, stopping the machine before the defect can continue to propagate. The associated defect image is displayed locally for operator fault diagnosis and is uploaded to the cloud platform for subsequent defect statistics and process improvement. After the operator resolves the event and restarts the machine, the production cycle resumes. The latency-critical defect-to-stop loop remains local and does not depend on cloud communication.

\subsection{On-Machine Deployment Configuration}
The deployed edge terminal uses a Rockchip RK3566 processor running Android~13. Images are acquired with a SmartSens 1.3-megapixel industrial high-definition global-shutter camera at $1280\times1024$ resolution and a nominal 30~frames/s. Adjustable supplementary lighting is used to stabilize the local imaging condition. The camera is installed 10~cm from the fabric, providing an approximately $16\times14$~cm field of view. On the deployed terminal, the reported inference time is 45~ms per processed image (approximately 22.2 processed frames/s). This rate is sufficient for the maximum stated machine condition: a 38-inch circular knitting machine at 30~revolutions/min has a circumference of approximately 3.03~m and a fabric-surface speed of approximately 1.52~m/s. Consequently, the surface advances approximately 6.8~cm during one 45-ms inference interval, which remains below the 14-cm shorter side of the field of view and yields at least 7.2~cm nominal overlap between adjacent processed views. The edge terminal is connected to the circular knitting machine control system by a direct wired interface; the measured time from a defect decision to transmission of the stop-control signal is below 10~ms. The edge terminal synchronizes non-latency-critical status and defect records with the cloud IoT platform through MQTT. The system is designed to support continuous 24/7 inspection. \subsection{Platform-Aware Baseline Selection and NPU Deployment}
The choice of YOLOv8n as the baseline is driven by deployment compatibility rather than by version chronology alone. The RKNN Model Zoo provides tested RK3566 NPU examples and single-core INT8 throughput figures for several YOLO families at $640\times640$ input \cite{rockchipmodelzoo}. In the current official benchmark, YOLOv8n reaches 34.0~frames/s, exceeding the listed newer YOLOv10n (20.7~frames/s) and YOLO11n (20.6~frames/s). The legacy YOLOv5n and YOLOv6n entries are faster (39.7 and 48.8~frames/s, respectively), which directly demonstrates that a newer YOLO version is not automatically a better NPU choice. We therefore select YOLOv8n as a high-throughput baseline with tested RK3566 NPU examples in the official Model Zoo and an extensible structure for textile-specific modification, rather than claiming that it is the absolute fastest model in the official benchmark. The benchmark values are used only for platform-aware model selection: they do not substitute for measuring the final customized detector on the deployed application.

The training and deployment models use different representations. Training produces a PyTorch checkpoint, which is exported at $640\times640$ through ONNX and then converted to an RKNN model for the RK3566 NPU. We use the official RKNN-Toolkit2 conversion workflow, which targets Rockchip NPUs and supports model conversion, quantization, inference, and performance evaluation \cite{rockchiptoolkit2}. Integer-only quantization is a well-established route to reduce inference cost on constrained hardware \cite{jacob2018quantization}. In the deployment path, post-training INT8 quantization is applied during ONNX-to-RKNN conversion with representative training images used for calibration; the resulting RKNN artifact is loaded by the Android edge application for NPU execution. This workflow is summarized as \emph{PyTorch checkpoint} $\rightarrow$ \emph{ONNX} $\rightarrow$ \emph{INT8 RKNN} $\rightarrow$ \emph{RK3566 NPU}. The reported 45-ms edge inference time in Table~\ref{tab:deployment} corresponds to the deployed application, not PyTorch inference on the training workstation.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cloud} shows the corresponding cloud IoT platform. The platform provides a portal view, defect-count trend statistics, device status and remote configuration, and a review page for uploaded defect records. These cloud functions support supervisory operation and process analysis; they are separated from the latency-critical local wired stop path.

The design follows the edge-intelligence principle that time-sensitive perception and action remain near the physical process, while selected evidence can be retained centrally \cite{shi2016edge}. The cloud side is organized as a manufacturing digital-twin service for live state visualization, statistics, and authorized supervision \cite{tao2019digitaltwin,kritzinger2018digitaltwin}. Here, the control consequence is more direct: the event is escalated to a worker, not accepted as a final autonomous verdict. This provides a pragmatic route for handling rare defects while retaining the records needed to identify recurring defect patterns and process anomalies.

\subsection{Operational Boundaries}
The workflow separates three stages: (i) whether machine rotation enables inspection, (ii) whether the detector produces a defect decision and initiates the local stop path, and (iii) how an operator diagnoses and disposes of the stopped-machine event. The reported recognition experiments validate only the second stage on a held-out image split. The deployed configuration establishes geometric image coverage at the stated maximum machine speed, the camera, edge terminal, wired control interface, and decision-to-command latency, but does not yet quantify physical machine-stop time, false-stop rate on defect-free fabric, frame-delivery or drop statistics during continuous operation, or operator-diagnosis completion rate. These measurements should be reported from a controlled site trial before submission.

\section{TFRA-YOLO}
\subsection{Overview}
\model is built from the YOLOv8n detector \cite{ultralytics2023}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:network}, the backbone retains the original resolution hierarchy, but replaces the C2f output refinement at four stages with C2f--DTE. In the head, the two top-down nearest-neighbor upsampling operations are replaced with LTFR. The standard concatenation, C2f fusion, bottom-up path, and three-scale detection head are retained. The detection scales are $P_3$, $P_4$, and $P_5$, corresponding to $80\times80$, $40\times40$, and $20\times20$ feature maps for a $640\times640$ input.

\subsection{Directional Texture Encoding}
The visual cue of a defect in knitted fabric is usually not an isolated object boundary. Instead, it is a local violation of the repetitive loop texture: a \emph{row} defect typically interrupts continuity across the width direction, a \emph{col} defect typically interrupts continuity along the height direction, and a \emph{hole} produces a compact local discontinuity. A standard C2f output preserves multi-path features and can learn such local patterns, but it does not explicitly introduce a directional decomposition for these three forms of texture interruption. DTE therefore uses horizontal, vertical, and local texture branches to encode the corresponding image-space structures.

DTE is inserted \emph{after} the selected C2f outputs rather than replacing the C2f gradient-flow structure. Thus, the original C2f features remain available through the residual pathway, while DTE learns a residual texture correction at multiple backbone depths. For a C2f output $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, the module applies depthwise horizontal, vertical, and local branches and then fuses them:
\begin{equation}
F(X)=\phi\left([\operatorname{DW}_{1\times7}(X),\operatorname{DW}_{7\times1}(X),\operatorname{DW}_{3\times3}(X)]\right),
\label{eq:dte}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation and $\phi$ is a $1\times1$ convolution with normalization and activation. The $1\times7$ branch aggregates evidence along a horizontal trace without first mixing channels, whereas the $7\times1$ branch performs the analogous vertical aggregation. The $3\times3$ branch retains compact local evidence required for holes and short texture breaks. Depthwise filtering keeps the long directional receptive fields inexpensive; the following pointwise fusion learns how much horizontal, vertical, and local evidence should be retained at each feature channel. Because input and output channels match in the present implementation, the module uses residual fusion:
\begin{equation}
Y=X+F(X).
\label{eq:dte-residual}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:dte-residual} is important for an edge-deployed adaptation: the C2f feature $X$ remains directly available if a directional response is unhelpful, while $F(X)$ adds only the learned, task-specific texture cue. The design is related to, but not equivalent to, asymmetric convolution blocks \cite{ding2019acnet}: it uses depthwise directional paths, three-way concatenation, and a learned pointwise fusion within the existing C2f structure. Its intended effect is therefore selective sensitivity to elongated horizontal and vertical discontinuities without sacrificing the compact context used for holes.

\subsection{Local Texture-Guided Feature Reassembly}
The features enriched by DTE must next be passed from a coarse semantic level to a finer detection level. Fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling copies a source value to a $2\times2$ region according to a content-independent rule; its reconstruction weights do not adapt to local texture structure. This can be suboptimal in the top-down feature-pyramid transitions \cite{lin2017fpn}, where coarse semantic features must be aligned with high-resolution texture evidence and narrow defect boundaries may occupy only a small fraction of the local region. LTFR therefore replaces only these two fixed upsampling operations; the original concatenation, C2f fusion, bottom-up path, and detection head remain unchanged.

LTFR instantiates the content-aware reassembly principle of CARAFE \cite{wang2019carafe} in this restricted pyramid role. Given $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, a $1\times1$ compressor first reduces the kernel-prediction feature to $C_r=\max(C/4,16)$ channels. A $3\times3$ encoder then produces $s^2k^2$ kernel logits, where the scale factor is $s=2$ and the reassembly kernel size is $k=5$. Pixel shuffle expands these logits onto the upsampled grid, and softmax yields a distinct normalized $5\times5$ kernel $K_{i,j}$ at each output location:
\begin{equation}
K_{i,j}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\operatorname{PS}\left(E_{3\times3}(E_{1\times1}(X))\right)_{i,j}\right).
\label{eq:ltfr-kernel}
\end{equation}
Here, $E_{1\times1}$ and $E_{3\times3}$ denote the compression and kernel-encoding layers, respectively, and $\operatorname{PS}$ denotes pixel shuffle. The $25$ weights of each $K_{i,j}$ sum to one. For each output position, a $5\times5$ neighborhood $\mathcal{N}_{i,j}(X)$ is unfolded from $X$, spatially aligned with the enlarged grid, and reassembled as
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,j}=\sum_{u=1}^{25}K_{i,j,u}\mathcal{N}_{i,j,u}(X).
\label{eq:ltfr-reassembly}
\end{equation}

Unlike a spatially shared interpolation kernel, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ltfr-reassembly} allows a location near a thin defect or a texture boundary to select a different local mixture from a location within regular fabric. The channel-compression stage bounds the overhead of predicting these kernels, while the $5\times5$ neighborhood provides more local support than a copied source pixel. LTFR is not claimed as a new generic reassembly primitive; its contribution here is the lightweight placement of input-dependent reassembly at the two texture-critical top-down transitions of YOLOv8n.

DTE and LTFR have complementary roles. DTE first creates direction-sensitive cues in the backbone; LTFR then propagates and aligns those cues during top-down reconstruction instead of indiscriminately replicating them. The component ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} tests this design: DTE produces the larger isolated gain, LTFR still improves the baseline on its own, and their combination yields the best result. Figs.~\ref{fig:dte} and~\ref{fig:ltfr} detail the two modules.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We use the COCO detection protocol \cite{lin2014coco}. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, intersection over union (IoU) is defined as $\operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=|B_p\cap B_g|/|B_p\cup B_g|$. At an IoU threshold $t$, a prediction is counted as a true positive only when it has the correct class and is matched one-to-one to an as-yet unmatched ground-truth instance; all remaining predictions are false positives and unmatched ground-truth instances are false negatives. For each class $c$, $\operatorname{AP}_c(t)$ is the area under its interpolated precision--recall curve. With $C=3$ classes, the reported AP and AP50 are
\begin{align}
\operatorname{AP} &= \frac{1}{10C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{AP}_c(t), \notag\\
\operatorname{AP50} &= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\operatorname{AP}_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{T}=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$. 
Thus, AP measures localization and classification quality across increasingly strict overlap thresholds, whereas AP50 emphasizes detection at IoU $=0.50$. Both are written as percentages in the tables. We additionally report trainable parameters and FLOPs at $[1,3,640,640]$ to characterize model scale and computational cost; these are not substitutes for measured edge-device latency.

For each model, the retained best-validation checkpoint is evaluated once on the same held-out test split. FLOPs and parameter counts are reported at $[1,3,640,640]$. The values are suitable for a coarse efficiency comparison, but cross-framework FLOP accounting can omit unsupported operators; no cross-framework latency claim is made. DETR, Deformable DETR, Faster R-CNN, and RT-DETRv2 follow their respective repositories and pretrained backbones \cite{carion2020detr,zhu2021deformable,ren2015faster,lv2024rtdetrv2}.

\subsection{Limitations and Submission-Ready Validation}
The present evidence supports a promising lightweight detector on a real production-line image set, but it does not yet establish production reliability. First, the test split is small and category-imbalanced. Second, FLOPs are not a substitute for end-to-end edge latency, especially when image capture, preprocessing, postprocessing, control communication, and physical stopping are part of the operational loop. Finally, operator diagnosis records can support defect statistics and process analysis, but their completeness and operational value have not yet been measured. These points define a concrete validation plan, rather than weaknesses that should be concealed.

\section{Conclusion}
This work presents a closed-loop framework for defect inspection on a circular knitting machine and \model, a compact direction- and content-adaptive detector. The workflow couples rotation-aware edge inspection, automatic local wired stop signaling, on-site operator inspection and fault diagnosis, and a cloud digital twin for live status visualization, defect statistics, process improvement, and authorized remote supervisory control. The detector combines DTE for horizontal, vertical, and local knitted-texture encoding with LTFR for adaptive feature-pyramid reconstruction. On the retained test split, \model improves YOLOv8n from 59.3\% to 66.3\% AP at 3.39 M parameters and 8.9 G FLOPs. The next step is not merely more offline accuracy: it is a disciplined site evaluation of sensing-to-stop latency, false-stop behavior, operator-diagnosis workflow, process-analysis value, and grouped generalization across production conditions.
