\begin{abstract}
Large circular knitting machines underpin high-throughput textile production, particularly for plain-knit fabric, one of the most extensively manufactured fabric types. Conventional inspection is commonly performed after production; therefore, a defect that emerges early can remain undiscovered while the machine continues knitting, causing a substantial length of defective fabric, material waste, and rework. This paper presents an in-process, closed-loop edge-cloud inspection workflow and TFRA-YOLO, a lightweight detector designed for this production setting. A proximity-switch rotation signal activates edge-side inspection; when a defect is detected, the edge device sends a local stop command and presents the candidate defect to an operator for secondary confirmation. Device states, defect records, and local confirmation outcomes are synchronized to a cloud digital-twin platform for real-time status visualization, defect statistics, process-improvement analysis, and authorized remote supervisory control. TFRA-YOLO starts from YOLOv8n and introduces a warp--weft texture encoding (WWTE) block that jointly models horizontal, vertical, and local texture responses, together with a local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) block that replaces the two fixed top-down upsampling operations with local feature reassembly. On a real production-line dataset of 403 images covering horizontal defects, vertical defects, and holes, the retained test split contains 81 images and 91 instances. TFRA-YOLO obtains 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving YOLOv8n by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively, with 3.39 M parameters and 8.9 G FLOPs at $640\times640$ input. These results support the usefulness of orthogonal texture representation and local pyramid reconstruction for lightweight, in-process plain-knit fabric inspection. The manuscript also states the deployment measurements still required before a production-performance claim is made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines produce fabric continuously, so an overlooked defect can propagate before an operator can intervene. A practical inspection system must therefore address more than offline recognition accuracy: it must synchronize sensing with machine operation, make a local decision without waiting for a cloud round trip, preserve evidence for local human review, and synchronize confirmed cases and device states to a cloud digital twin for status visualization, defect statistics, and process improvement. Earlier vision systems have demonstrated on-machine inspection for circular knitting processes \cite{saeidi2005vision}, while recent work continues to identify rapid motion, illumination variation, and scarce abnormal samples as central challenges \cite{ni2025fast}. Broader reviews trace the evolution of fabric inspection from handcrafted texture analysis to learning-based methods and emphasize the simultaneous demands of localization accuracy, limited defect labels, and production-line speed \cite{kumar2008survey,ngan2011review,li2021survey,kahraman2023review}. CNN-based localization \cite{jing2019cnn}, weakly supervised detection \cite{liu2021dlse}, and recent lightweight detectors for circular weft fabric and edge devices \cite{kang2024ayolov7,yang2024yolov4tinys} each address parts of this problem. However, many learning-based defect-detection studies still evaluate an image classifier or detector in isolation from the production response loop.

The target images in this work exhibit a second challenge. The fabric surface is dominated by regular knitted texture, whereas a horizontal defect, a vertical defect, or a hole can be thin, low contrast, and highly anisotropic. One-stage detectors, from the original YOLO formulation \cite{redmon2016yolo} through anchor-free and trainable-efficiency variants \cite{ge2021yolox,wang2023yolov7}, provide a suitable starting point when the detector must remain compact. A compact detector must nevertheless retain local texture detail at the high-resolution pyramid level while also exploiting context to distinguish a true defect from benign texture changes. General multi-scale detectors use feature pyramids \cite{lin2017fpn} and bottom-up aggregation \cite{liu2018panet}; content-aware feature reassembly \cite{wang2019carafe} further shows that interpolation need not use a spatially fixed kernel. At the same time, asymmetric convolutional paths can strengthen directional structure \cite{ding2019acnet}. These ideas are relevant to knitted fabric, but must be adapted conservatively so that the edge model remains small.

At the system level, edge computing reduces dependence on remote-cloud communication for latency-sensitive decisions \cite{shi2016edge}, while digital-twin studies distinguish data-connected virtual representations from the local physical process they supervise \cite{tao2019digitaltwin,kritzinger2018digitaltwin}. Accordingly, the validation plan in this work combines detection accuracy, model complexity, qualitative inspection, and embedded deployment measurements rather than relying on an accuracy-only presentation.

This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It formulates a production-oriented, human-in-the-loop inspection workflow for a circular knitting machine. A rotation-derived signal gates edge inspection, a local defect decision requests a stop, an on-site operator confirms the case, and device states plus detection-confirmation records are synchronized to a cloud digital twin for real-time visualization, defect statistics, process-improvement analysis, and authorized remote supervisory control.
  \item It proposes \model, a YOLOv8n-based detector with a warp--weft texture encoding (WWTE) block and a local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) block. WWTE preserves horizontal, vertical, and local texture cues; LTFR performs content-adaptive rather than fixed-neighbor top-down reconstruction.
  \item It reports a transparent test-set comparison against lightweight YOLO baselines and representative two-stage and transformer detectors, together with a component ablation. The retained result improves AP from 59.3\% for YOLOv8n to 66.3\% for \model at a modest 0.38 M-parameter and 0.8 G-FLOP increase.
\end{itemize}

Unlike a fully autonomous control claim, the proposed workflow deliberately retains on-site operator confirmation. The cloud digital-twin platform provides live equipment-state visualization, defect analytics, and authorized remote supervisory control. The latency-critical defect-to-stop path remains local and wired; it does not depend on a cloud return path. The system is not claimed to perform unattended online learning or safety-certified machine control.

\section{Closed-Loop Inspection Workflow}
\subsection{Production Workflow}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} illustrates the intended operating sequence. An operator starts the circular knitting machine. A proximity switch reports the machine rotation state to the edge side and enables image acquisition and inference. The edge unit analyzes captured fabric images locally. When its decision logic identifies a candidate defect, it sends a stop request through the machine-side control interface and records the associated image and prediction. The candidate is shown locally to the operator for secondary confirmation. After the operator handles the event and restarts the machine, the production cycle resumes. Device states, detection records, and local confirmation outcomes are synchronized to a cloud digital-twin platform, where they support real-time status visualization, defect-category statistics, and process-improvement analysis. Authorized users can also issue remote supervisory control actions through the platform; these actions are distinct from the local wired defect-to-stop path.

\subsection{On-Machine Deployment Configuration}
The deployed edge terminal uses a Rockchip RK3566 processor running Android~13. Images are acquired with a SmartSens 1.3-megapixel industrial high-definition global-shutter camera at $1280\times1024$ resolution and a nominal 30~frames/s. The camera is installed 10~cm from the fabric, providing an approximately $16\times14$~cm field of view. On the deployed terminal, the reported inference time is 45~ms per processed image (approximately 22.2 processed frames/s). This rate is sufficient for the maximum stated machine condition: a 38-inch circular knitting machine at 30~revolutions/min has a circumference of approximately 3.03~m and a fabric-surface speed of approximately 1.52~m/s. Consequently, the surface advances approximately 6.8~cm during one 45-ms inference interval, which remains below the 14-cm shorter side of the field of view and yields at least 7.2~cm nominal overlap between adjacent processed views. The edge terminal is connected to the circular knitting machine control system by a direct wired interface; the measured time from a defect decision to transmission of the stop-control signal is below 10~ms. This value characterizes the electrical control-command path, not the physical time required for the machine to come to a full stop. The system is designed to support continuous 24/7 inspection. \subsection{Platform-Aware Baseline Selection and NPU Deployment}
The choice of YOLOv8n as the baseline is driven by deployment compatibility rather than by version chronology alone. The RKNN Model Zoo provides tested RK3566 NPU examples and single-core INT8 throughput figures for several YOLO families at $640\times640$ input \cite{rockchipmodelzoo}. In the current official benchmark, YOLOv8n reaches 34.0~frames/s, exceeding the listed newer YOLOv10n (20.7~frames/s) and YOLO11n (20.6~frames/s). The legacy YOLOv5n and YOLOv6n entries are faster (39.7 and 48.8~frames/s, respectively), which directly demonstrates that a newer YOLO version is not automatically a better NPU choice. We therefore select YOLOv8n as a high-throughput, officially supported, and structurally extensible baseline for textile-specific modification, rather than claiming that it is the absolute fastest model in the official benchmark. The benchmark values are used only for platform-aware model selection: they do not substitute for measuring the final customized detector on the deployed application.

The training and deployment models use different representations. Training produces a PyTorch checkpoint, which is exported at $640\times640$ through ONNX and then converted to an RKNN model for the RK3566 NPU. We use the official RKNN-Toolkit2 conversion workflow, which targets Rockchip NPUs and supports model conversion, quantization, inference, and performance evaluation \cite{rockchiptoolkit2}. Integer-only quantization is a well-established route to reduce inference cost on constrained hardware \cite{jacob2018quantization}. In the deployment path, post-training INT8 quantization is applied during ONNX-to-RKNN conversion with representative training images used for calibration; the resulting RKNN artifact is loaded by the Android edge application for NPU execution. This workflow is summarized as \emph{PyTorch checkpoint} $\rightarrow$ \emph{ONNX} $\rightarrow$ \emph{INT8 RKNN} $\rightarrow$ \emph{RK3566 NPU}. The reported 45-ms edge inference time in Table~\ref{tab:deployment} corresponds to the deployed application, not PyTorch inference on the training workstation.

The upper inset highlights the camera directed at the knitting region, and the lower inset shows the edge-side touch display during inspection. Together with the workflow in Fig.~\ref{fig:system}, this installation evidence distinguishes the work from an offline-only image experiment.

The design follows the edge-intelligence principle that time-sensitive perception and action remain near the physical process, while selected evidence can be retained centrally. Here, the control consequence is more direct: the event is escalated to a worker, not accepted as a final autonomous verdict. This provides a pragmatic route for handling rare defects while retaining the records needed to identify recurring defect patterns and process anomalies.

\subsection{Operational Boundaries}
The workflow separates three decisions: (i) whether machine rotation enables inspection, (ii) whether the detector produces a candidate defect, and (iii) whether an operator confirms and disposes of the event. The reported recognition experiments validate only the second decision on a held-out image split. The deployed configuration establishes geometric image coverage at the stated maximum machine speed, the camera, edge terminal, wired control interface, and decision-to-command latency, but does not yet quantify physical machine-stop time, false-stop rate on defect-free fabric, frame-delivery or drop statistics during continuous operation, or operator-confirmation rate. These measurements should be reported from a controlled site trial before submission.

\section{TFRA-YOLO}
\subsection{Overview}
\model is built from the YOLOv8n detector \cite{ultralytics2023}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:network}, the backbone retains the original resolution hierarchy, but replaces the C2f output refinement at four stages with C2f--WWTE. In the head, the two top-down nearest-neighbor upsampling operations are replaced with LTFR. The standard concatenation, C2f fusion, bottom-up path, and three-scale detection head are retained. The detection scales are $P_3$, $P_4$, and $P_5$, corresponding to $80\times80$, $40\times40$, and $20\times20$ feature maps for a $640\times640$ input.

\subsection{Warp--Weft Texture Encoding}
The visual cue of a defect in plain-knit fabric is usually not an isolated object boundary. Instead, it is a local violation of the repetitive loop texture: a horizontal defect interrupts continuity across the width direction, a vertical defect interrupts continuity along the height direction, and a hole produces a compact local discontinuity. A standard C2f output preserves multi-path features, but its local convolutions do not explicitly distinguish these three forms of texture interruption. The term ``warp--weft'' is retained as the internal WWTE name because it denotes two orthogonal texture directions. In this circular-knit application, it does \emph{not} imply that the fabric is woven; operationally, the branches are interpreted as horizontal and vertical texture encoders.

WWTE is inserted \emph{after} the selected C2f outputs rather than replacing the C2f gradient-flow structure. Thus, the original pretrained representation is retained, while WWTE learns a residual texture correction at multiple backbone depths. For a C2f output $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, the module applies depthwise horizontal, vertical, and local branches and then fuses them:
\begin{equation}
F(X)=\phi\left([\operatorname{DW}_{1\times7}(X),\operatorname{DW}_{7\times1}(X),\operatorname{DW}_{3\times3}(X)]\right),
\label{eq:wwte}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]$ denotes channel concatenation and $\phi$ is a $1\times1$ convolution with normalization and activation. The $1\times7$ branch aggregates evidence along a horizontal trace without first mixing channels, whereas the $7\times1$ branch performs the analogous vertical aggregation. The $3\times3$ branch retains compact local evidence required for holes and short texture breaks. Depthwise filtering keeps the long directional receptive fields inexpensive; the following pointwise fusion learns how much horizontal, vertical, and local evidence should be retained at each feature channel. Because input and output channels match in the present implementation, the module uses residual fusion:
\begin{equation}
Y=X+F(X).
\label{eq:wwte-residual}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:wwte-residual} is important for an edge-deployed adaptation: the C2f feature $X$ remains directly available if a directional response is unhelpful, while $F(X)$ adds only the learned, task-specific texture cue. The design is related to, but not equivalent to, asymmetric convolution blocks \cite{ding2019acnet}: it uses depthwise directional paths, three-way concatenation, and a learned pointwise fusion within the existing C2f structure. Its intended effect is therefore selective sensitivity to elongated horizontal and vertical discontinuities without sacrificing the compact context used for holes.

\subsection{Local Texture-Guided Feature Reassembly}
The features enriched by WWTE must next be passed from a coarse semantic level to a finer detection level. Fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling simply copies a source value to a $2\times2$ region; it has no mechanism to decide whether the copied response corresponds to regular fabric texture, a thin directional break, or a defect boundary. This limitation is most consequential in the two top-down transitions, where coarse semantic features must be aligned with the high-resolution texture evidence used by the $P_4$ and $P_3$ detectors. LTFR therefore replaces only these two fixed upsampling operations; the original concatenation, C2f fusion, bottom-up path, and detection head remain unchanged.

LTFR instantiates the content-aware reassembly principle of CARAFE \cite{wang2019carafe} in this restricted pyramid role. Given $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, a $1\times1$ compressor first reduces the kernel-prediction feature to $C_r=\max(C/4,16)$ channels. A $3\times3$ encoder then produces $s^2k^2$ kernel logits, where the scale factor is $s=2$ and the reassembly kernel size is $k=5$. Pixel shuffle expands these logits onto the upsampled grid, and softmax yields a distinct normalized $5\times5$ kernel $K_{i,j}$ at each output location:
\begin{equation}
K_{i,j}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\operatorname{PS}\left(E_{3\times3}(E_{1\times1}(X))\right)_{i,j}\right).
\label{eq:ltfr-kernel}
\end{equation}
Here, $E_{1\times1}$ and $E_{3\times3}$ denote the compression and kernel-encoding layers, respectively, and $\operatorname{PS}$ denotes pixel shuffle. The $25$ weights of each $K_{i,j}$ sum to one. For each output position, a $5\times5$ neighborhood $\mathcal{N}_{i,j}(X)$ is unfolded from $X$, spatially aligned with the enlarged grid, and reassembled as
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,j}=\sum_{u=1}^{25}K_{i,j,u}\mathcal{N}_{i,j,u}(X).
\label{eq:ltfr-reassembly}
\end{equation}

Unlike a spatially shared interpolation kernel, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ltfr-reassembly} allows a location near a thin defect or a texture boundary to select a different local mixture from a location within regular fabric. The channel-compression stage bounds the overhead of predicting these kernels, while the $5\times5$ neighborhood provides more local support than a copied source pixel. LTFR is not claimed as a new generic reassembly primitive; its contribution here is the lightweight placement of input-dependent reassembly at the two texture-critical top-down transitions of YOLOv8n.

WWTE and LTFR have complementary roles. WWTE first creates direction-sensitive cues in the backbone; LTFR then propagates and aligns those cues during top-down reconstruction instead of indiscriminately replicating them. The component ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} tests this design: WWTE produces the larger isolated gain, LTFR still improves the baseline on its own, and their combination yields the best result. Figs.~\ref{fig:wwte} and~\ref{fig:ltfr} detail the two modules.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We use the COCO detection protocol. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, intersection over union (IoU) is defined as $\operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=|B_p\cap B_g|/|B_p\cup B_g|$. At an IoU threshold $t$, a prediction is counted as a true positive only when it has the correct class and is matched one-to-one to an as-yet unmatched ground-truth instance; all remaining predictions are false positives and unmatched ground-truth instances are false negatives. For each class $c$, $\operatorname{AP}_c(t)$ is the area under its interpolated precision--recall curve. With $C=3$ classes, the reported AP and AP50 are
\begin{align}
\operatorname{AP} &= \frac{1}{10C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{AP}_c(t), \notag\\
\operatorname{AP50} &= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\operatorname{AP}_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{T}=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$. 
Thus, AP measures localization and classification quality across increasingly strict overlap thresholds, whereas AP50 emphasizes detection at IoU $=0.50$. Both are written as percentages in the tables. We additionally report trainable parameters and FLOPs at $[1,3,640,640]$ to characterize model scale and computational cost; these are not substitutes for measured edge-device latency.

For each model, the retained best-validation checkpoint is evaluated once on the same held-out test split. FLOPs and parameter counts are reported at $[1,3,640,640]$. The values are suitable for a coarse efficiency comparison, but cross-framework FLOP accounting can omit unsupported operators; no cross-framework latency claim is made. DETR, Deformable DETR, Faster R-CNN, and RT-DETRv2 follow their respective repositories and pretrained backbones \cite{carion2020detr,zhu2021deformable,ren2015faster,lv2024rtdetrv2}.

\subsection{Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates the two modifications within the same YOLOv8n family. WWTE contributes the larger isolated improvement (+4.8 AP), while LTFR contributes +1.6 AP. Combining both gives 66.3\% AP, which is 0.6 points above the arithmetic sum of the isolated gains. This indicates that the texture representation and adaptive reassembly are complementary: WWTE supplies direction-specific texture cues, whereas LTFR transfers them through the top-down path.

\subsection{Limitations and Submission-Ready Validation}
The present evidence supports a promising lightweight detector on a real production-line image set, but it does not yet establish production reliability. First, the test split is small and category-imbalanced. Second, FLOPs are not a substitute for end-to-end edge latency, especially when image capture, preprocessing, postprocessing, control communication, and physical stopping are part of the operational loop. Finally, the operator's confirmation records are intended to improve defect statistics and process analysis; their actual acceptance rate and operational value have not yet been measured. These points define a concrete validation plan, rather than weaknesses that should be concealed.

\section{Conclusion}
This work presents a closed-loop framework for defect inspection on a circular knitting machine and \model, a compact direction- and content-adaptive detector. The workflow couples rotation-aware edge inspection, local wired stop signaling, on-site human confirmation, and a cloud digital twin for live status visualization, defect statistics, process improvement, and authorized remote supervisory control. The detector combines WWTE for horizontal, vertical, and local knitted-texture encoding with LTFR for adaptive feature-pyramid reconstruction. On the retained test split, \model improves YOLOv8n from 59.3\% to 66.3\% AP at 3.39 M parameters and 8.9 G FLOPs. The next step is not merely more offline accuracy: it is a disciplined site evaluation of sensing-to-stop latency, false-stop behavior, confirmation outcomes, process-analysis value, and grouped generalization across production conditions.
