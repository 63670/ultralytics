\begin{abstract}
Circular knitting machines are foundational production infrastructure in the textile industry. A fabric defect arising during production can propagate during continuous knitting and cause substantial fabric loss if it is not detected and handled promptly. To address this issue, we design and implement a cloud-edge collaborative inspection system integrating a Hall sensor, adjustable supplementary lighting, an industrial high-definition camera, an RK3566 edge terminal, a textile-specific detector, and an IoT cloud platform. When the Hall sensor indicates that the machine is operating, the edge terminal activates the camera and supplementary light. Once a defect is detected, it stops the machine through the local wired control path and reports the defect to the cloud platform for defect statistics and production-quality analysis. The proposed TFRA-YOLO starts from YOLOv8n and combines directional texture encoding (DTE) with local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) to retain directional knitted-texture cues and adaptively reconstruct the feature pyramid. On images collected from a real textile production line, TFRA-YOLO achieves 66.3\% AP and 96.9\% AP50, improving YOLOv8n by 7.0 and 2.8 percentage points, respectively. The proposed system provides a practical route from delayed post-production inspection toward traceable, in-process defect containment for circular knitting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines are foundational production infrastructure in the textile industry: they continuously manufacture the knitted fabrics used in T-shirts, sportswear, and other daily garments. The latest International Textile Manufacturers Federation (ITMF) statistics report 28,000 global shipments of large circular knitting machines in 2024 \cite{itmf2024itmss}. Conventional inspection is commonly performed after production. Consequently, a defect arising early in production may continue propagating as the machine operates, leading to substantial fabric waste before it is discovered. An effective inspection system must therefore detect and localize defects during machine operation with sufficiently low latency to enable timely intervention before defective fabric continues to accumulate.

Single-stage YOLO detectors are particularly attractive for this setting because they jointly provide real-time inference and explicit defect localization. YOLO-based detectors have been adopted for industrial surface inspection of steel strips, steel surfaces, and large stamped parts \cite{gao2024srnyolo,li2024yoloxsteel,dong2024snf}. In fabric-defect detection, existing studies have adapted YOLO through lightweight or tiny-model designs \cite{kang2024ayolov7,yang2024yolov4tinys,chen2025lwfdd}, adaptive or deformable feature extraction \cite{luo2023deformableyolo,zhang2025dghr}, and enhanced multi-scale fusion or prediction for weak and small defects \cite{yue2022tiny,yu2024fayolo,chen2025yolov8,zhou2025dcfe,gao2026yolov8,xu2025yolodll,wang2025srseyolo}. These studies establish the suitability of the YOLO route for bounding-box localization under industrial latency and resource constraints. However, most existing modifications primarily strengthen generic feature representations rather than explicitly exploiting the directional texture structure of circular-knitted fabrics.

For circular-knitted fabric inspection, two texture-specific limitations remain in conventional YOLO feature encoding and top-down reconstruction. First, circular-knitted fabrics exhibit highly repetitive texture patterns with dominant horizontal and vertical orientations. Defects typically disrupt this regular texture in horizontally extended, vertically extended, or compact local forms such as holes. Standard convolutional feature extraction can learn these patterns implicitly, but it does not explicitly introduce an orientation-aware inductive bias for separately modeling directional and compact texture disruptions. Second, nearest-neighbor upsampling in the top-down feature pyramid reconstructs higher-resolution features with a content-independent copying rule rather than adapting reconstruction weights to local texture structure. Such content-independent reconstruction may inadequately preserve narrow defect boundaries or weak local disruptions embedded in repetitive texture. These observations motivate explicit directional texture encoding during feature extraction and content-adaptive reconstruction during top-down fusion.

Based on this motivation, we develop \model to introduce texture-specific inductive biases at both feature encoding and feature reconstruction stages. To address the first limitation, we introduce a directional texture encoding (DTE) module after selected backbone stages. DTE separately models horizontal, vertical, and compact local texture evidence, while residual fusion retains the baseline representation and augments it with directional texture cues. To address the second limitation, we introduce a local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) pyramid that replaces fixed nearest-neighbor top-down upsampling with location-specific feature reassembly. \model preserves the lightweight single-stage YOLO detection paradigm while introducing directional texture encoding in the backbone and content-adaptive reconstruction in the top-down feature pyramid.

Beyond detector design, practical deployment on circular knitting machines requires inspection to be synchronized with machine rotation and to support low-latency local intervention. We therefore integrate TFRA-YOLO into a cloud-edge inspection system that combines image acquisition synchronized with machine rotation, RK3566-based edge inference, and cloud-based IoT monitoring. The latency-critical intervention loop remains entirely local, while defect records and device states are synchronized with the cloud IoT platform for status visualization and production-quality analysis.

The main contributions of our work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce a directional texture encoding (DTE) module that explicitly models horizontal, vertical, and compact local texture cues and fuses them with the baseline backbone representation, providing a lightweight texture-specific inductive bias for circular-knitted defect detection.
  \item We develop a local texture-guided feature reassembly (LTFR) pyramid that replaces fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling with location-adaptive reconstruction, enabling content-adaptive reconstruction of local defect structures during top-down multi-scale fusion.
  \item We implement a cloud-edge inspection prototype that keeps the latency-critical intervention loop local while supporting centralized defect statistics and device supervision.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\subsection{TFRA-YOLO Architecture}
\model is built on YOLOv8n\cite{ultralytics2023} and consists of three main components: a directional texture encoding backbone, a local texture-guided reassembly pyramid, and a multi-scale detection head. YOLOv8n is selected as the baseline because it provides favorable inference efficiency on the target RK3566 platform while retaining sufficient architectural flexibility for texture-specific modification.\cite{rockchipmodelzoo} As shown in Fig. 1, the directional texture encoding backbone introduces DTE after selected C2f stages to strengthen the representation of horizontally extended, vertically extended, and compact local texture disruptions. The local texture-guided reassembly pyramid replaces the fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling operations with LTFR, allowing higher-resolution features to be reconstructed according to local content before multi-scale fusion. The fused pyramid features are then forwarded to the detection head for defect classification and localization.
The two proposed components target different stages of feature processing. DTE focuses on extracting direction-sensitive texture cues from the repetitive knitted background, whereas LTFR focuses on reconstructing and propagating these cues across feature scales.

\subsection{Directional Texture Encoding}
The visual cue of a defect in circular-knitted fabric is often a local disruption of repetitive texture rather than an isolated object boundary. Such disruptions commonly appear in horizontally elongated, vertically elongated, or compact local forms. A standard C2f block can learn these patterns implicitly through local feature aggregation, but it does not explicitly separate directional and compact texture responses. DTE is therefore introduced to encode these complementary structures with horizontal, vertical, and local branches.
For a C2f output $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, DTE first forms three depthwise branch responses:
\begin{align}
F_h &= \operatorname{DW}_{1\times7}(X), \label{eq:dte-horizontal}\\
F_v &= \operatorname{DW}_{7\times1}(X), \label{eq:dte-vertical}\\
F_l &= \operatorname{DW}_{3\times3}(X). \label{eq:dte-local}
\end{align}
The \(1\times7\) and \(7\times1\) branches enlarge the receptive field along the horizontal and vertical directions, respectively, while the \(3\times3\) branch preserves compact local texture information. The three responses are concatenated and fused by a \(1\times1\) convolution:
\begin{equation}
F_d=\phi\left([F_h,F_v,F_l]\right),
\label{eq:dte-fusion}
\end{equation}
where \([\cdot]\) denotes channel concatenation and \(\phi\) denotes pointwise convolution followed by normalization and activation. Depthwise filtering keeps the directional branches lightweight, while the pointwise operation mixes the branch responses across channels.
The fused response is added to the original C2f feature through a residual connection:
\begin{equation}
Y=X+F_d.
\label{eq:dte-residual}
\end{equation}

This preserves the baseline representation while introducing additional direction-sensitive texture cues with limited computational overhead. Although DTE is related to asymmetric convolution [19], it differs in using depthwise horizontal, vertical, and local branches together with three-way fusion and residual integration within the C2f backbone.

\subsection{Local Texture-Guided Feature Reassembly}
In the feature pyramid, fixed nearest-neighbor upsampling reconstructs higher-resolution features using a content-independent copying rule, without adapting to local texture structure. This can be suboptimal for multi-scale fusion in repetitive knitted textures, where narrow defect boundaries may occupy only a small portion of the local region. LTFR therefore replaces the two fixed upsampling operations with content-adaptive feature reassembly.

LTFR adopts content-aware feature reassembly to reconstruct each output location from a locally predicted neighborhood. This formulation follows the content-aware reassembly principle introduced in CARAFE \cite{wang2019carafe}. Given $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, a $1\times1$ compressor first reduces the kernel-prediction feature to $C_r=\max(C/4,16)$ channels:
\begin{equation}
X_c=E_{1\times1}(X).
\label{eq:ltfr-compression}
\end{equation}
A $3\times3$ encoder then predicts $s^2k^2$ kernel logits, where the scale factor is $s=2$ and the reassembly kernel size is $k=5$:
\begin{equation}
K'=E_{3\times3}(X_c).
\label{eq:ltfr-logits}
\end{equation}
Pixel shuffle expands the logits to the upsampled grid,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{K}=\operatorname{PS}(K'),
\label{eq:ltfr-pixelshuffle}
\end{equation}
and a softmax across the $k^2$ kernel positions produces a distinct normalized reassembly kernel at each output location:
\begin{equation}
K_{i,j}=\operatorname{softmax}(\widehat{K}_{i,j}).
\label{eq:ltfr-kernel}
\end{equation}
The normalization constraint is
\begin{equation}
\sum_{u=1}^{k^2}K_{i,j,u}=1.
\label{eq:ltfr-normalization}
\end{equation}
Here, $E_{1\times1}$ and $E_{3\times3}$ denote the compression and kernel-encoding layers, respectively, and $\operatorname{PS}$ denotes pixel shuffle. In parallel, a local $k\times k$ feature patch is extracted from $X$ and aligned with the enlarged grid:
\begin{equation}
P_{i,j}=\operatorname{Unfold}_{k\times k}(X)_{i,j}.
\label{eq:ltfr-patch}
\end{equation}
where $P_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times k^2}$ denotes the local feature neighborhood associated with output location $(i,j)$. The reassembled feature is then obtained by the location-specific weighted sum
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,j}=\sum_{u=1}^{k^2}K_{i,j,u}P_{i,j,u}.
\label{eq:ltfr-reassembly}
\end{equation}

Unlike a spatially shared interpolation kernel, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ltfr-reassembly} allows the reconstruction weights to vary with local texture content, so defect boundaries and regular knitted regions can be aggregated differently. The channel-compression stage bounds the overhead of predicting these kernels, while the $5\times5$ neighborhood provides more local support than a copied source pixel. In \model, this reassembly mechanism is applied specifically to the two feature-pyramid upsampling stages to preserve local defect structure during multi-scale fusion.

DTE and LTFR play complementary roles: DTE enhances direction-sensitive texture representation in the backbone, while LTFR performs content-adaptive reconstruction of these features across pyramid scales.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Inspection System}
\label{sec:deployment}
\paragraph{Production workflow.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the inspection system installed on a circular knitting machine, which comprises a Hall sensor, an industrial camera, and an RK3566 edge device. The workflow begins when the operator presses the machine's native start button, initiating rotation. The Hall sensor detects the rotation and signals the RK3566, which activates LED illumination, acquires video frames from the camera, and begins real-time defect inspection. Once a defect is detected, the RK3566 issues a stop command via a wired connection to the machine controller, halting production before the defect propagates further. The corresponding defect frame is displayed locally for on-site operator inspection and troubleshooting and uploaded to the cloud platform for defect statistics and production-quality analysis. After the operator resolves the issue and restarts the machine, the production cycle resumes. All defect-to-stop decisions are made locally, without relying on cloud communication.

\paragraph{On-machine deployment.}
The deployed edge terminal uses a Rockchip RK3566 processor running Android~13. Images are acquired with a SmartSens 1.3-megapixel industrial high-definition global-shutter camera at $1280\times1024$ resolution and a nominal 30~frames/s. Adjustable supplementary lighting is used to stabilize the local imaging condition. The camera is installed 10~cm from the fabric, providing an approximately $16\times14$~cm field of view. On the deployed terminal, the reported inference time is 45~ms per processed image (approximately 22.2 processed frames/s). This rate is sufficient for the maximum stated machine condition: a 38-inch circular knitting machine at 30~revolutions/min has a circumference of approximately 3.03~m and a fabric-surface speed of approximately 1.52~m/s. Consequently, the surface advances approximately 6.8~cm during one 45-ms inference interval, which remains below the 14-cm shorter side of the field of view and yields at least 7.2~cm nominal overlap between adjacent processed views. The edge terminal is connected to the circular knitting machine control system by a direct wired interface; the measured time from a defect decision to transmission of the stop-control signal is below 10~ms. The edge terminal synchronizes non-latency-critical status and defect records with the cloud IoT platform through MQTT. The system is designed to support continuous 24/7 inspection. \paragraph{Platform-aware deployment.}
The choice of YOLOv8n as the baseline is driven by deployment compatibility rather than by version chronology alone. The RKNN Model Zoo provides tested RK3566 NPU examples and single-core INT8 throughput figures for several YOLO families at $640\times640$ input \cite{rockchipmodelzoo}. In the current official benchmark, YOLOv8n reaches 34.0~frames/s, exceeding the listed newer YOLOv10n (20.7~frames/s) and YOLO11n (20.6~frames/s). The legacy YOLOv5n and YOLOv6n entries are faster (39.7 and 48.8~frames/s, respectively), which directly demonstrates that a newer YOLO version is not automatically a better NPU choice. We therefore select YOLOv8n as a high-throughput baseline with tested RK3566 NPU examples in the official Model Zoo and an extensible structure for textile-specific modification, rather than claiming that it is the absolute fastest model in the official benchmark. The benchmark values are used only for platform-aware model selection: they do not substitute for measuring the final customized detector on the deployed application.

Training produces a PyTorch checkpoint, which is exported at $640\times640$ through ONNX and then converted to an RKNN model for the RK3566 NPU. We use the official RKNN-Toolkit2 conversion workflow, which targets Rockchip NPUs and supports model conversion, quantization, inference, and performance evaluation \cite{rockchiptoolkit2}. Integer-only quantization is a well-established route to reduce inference cost on constrained hardware \cite{jacob2018quantization}. In the deployment path, post-training INT8 quantization is applied during ONNX-to-RKNN conversion with representative training images used for calibration; the resulting RKNN artifact is loaded by the Android edge application for NPU execution. This workflow is summarized as \emph{PyTorch checkpoint} $\rightarrow$ \emph{ONNX} $\rightarrow$ \emph{INT8 RKNN} $\rightarrow$ \emph{RK3566 NPU}. The reported 45-ms edge inference time in Table~\ref{tab:deployment} corresponds to the deployed application, not PyTorch inference on the training workstation.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cloud} shows the corresponding cloud IoT platform. The platform provides a portal view, defect-count trend statistics, device status and remote configuration, and a review page for uploaded defect records. These cloud functions support supervisory operation and production-quality analysis; they are separated from the latency-critical local wired stop path.

The design follows the edge-intelligence principle that time-sensitive perception and action remain near the physical process, while selected evidence can be retained centrally \cite{shi2016edge}. The cloud side is organized as a manufacturing digital-twin service for live state visualization, statistics, and authorized supervision \cite{tao2019digitaltwin,kritzinger2018digitaltwin}. Here, the control consequence is more direct: the event is escalated to a worker, not accepted as a final autonomous verdict. This provides a pragmatic route for handling rare defects while retaining the records needed to identify recurring defect patterns and production-quality issues.

\paragraph{Operational boundaries.}
The workflow separates three stages: (i) whether machine rotation enables inspection, (ii) whether the detector produces a defect decision and initiates the local stop path, and (iii) how an operator inspects and troubleshoots the stopped-machine event. The reported recognition experiments validate only the second stage on a held-out image split. The deployed configuration establishes geometric image coverage at the stated maximum machine speed, the camera, edge terminal, wired control interface, and decision-to-command latency, but does not yet quantify physical machine-stop time, false-stop rate on defect-free fabric, frame-delivery or drop statistics during continuous operation, or operator-troubleshooting completion rate. These measurements should be reported from a controlled site trial before submission.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We use the COCO detection protocol \cite{lin2014coco}. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, intersection over union (IoU) is defined as $\operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=|B_p\cap B_g|/|B_p\cup B_g|$. At an IoU threshold $t$, a prediction is counted as a true positive only when it has the correct class and is matched one-to-one to an as-yet unmatched ground-truth instance; all remaining predictions are false positives and unmatched ground-truth instances are false negatives. For each class $c$, $\operatorname{AP}_c(t)$ is the area under its interpolated precision--recall curve. With $C=3$ classes, the reported AP and AP50 are
\begin{align}
\operatorname{AP} &= \frac{1}{10C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{AP}_c(t), \notag\\
\operatorname{AP50} &= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\operatorname{AP}_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{T}=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$. 
Thus, AP measures localization and classification quality across increasingly strict overlap thresholds, whereas AP50 emphasizes detection at IoU $=0.50$. Both are written as percentages in the tables. We additionally report trainable parameters and FLOPs at $[1,3,640,640]$ to characterize model scale and computational cost; these are not substitutes for measured edge-device latency.

For each model, the retained best-validation checkpoint is evaluated once on the same held-out test split. FLOPs and parameter counts are reported at $[1,3,640,640]$. The values are suitable for a coarse efficiency comparison, but cross-framework FLOP accounting can omit unsupported operators; no cross-framework latency claim is made. DETR, Deformable DETR, Faster R-CNN, and RT-DETRv2 follow their respective repositories and pretrained backbones \cite{carion2020detr,zhu2021deformable,ren2015faster,lv2024rtdetrv2}.

\subsection{Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates the two modifications within the same YOLOv8n family. DTE contributes the larger isolated improvement (+4.8 AP), while LTFR contributes +1.6 AP. Combining both gives 66.3\% AP, which is 0.6 points above the arithmetic sum of the isolated gains. This indicates that the texture representation and adaptive reassembly are complementary: DTE supplies direction-specific texture cues, whereas LTFR transfers them through the top-down path. The current study establishes module-level complementarity; branch-level DTE and alternative-upsampling ablations are required before making more granular claims about individual directional branches, the directional kernel length, or the choice of content-adaptive reassembly.

\subsection{Limitations and Submission-Ready Validation}
The present evidence supports a promising lightweight detector on a real production-line image set, but it does not yet establish production reliability. First, the test split is small and category-imbalanced, and its image-level partition does not establish isolation of adjacent video frames. Second, FLOPs are not a substitute for end-to-end edge latency, especially when image capture, preprocessing, postprocessing, control communication, and physical stopping are part of the operational loop. Third, the current ablation isolates the two modules but does not yet separately quantify each DTE branch or compare LTFR with alternative interpolation and reassembly operators. Finally, operator troubleshooting records can support defect statistics and production-quality analysis, but their completeness and operational value have not yet been measured. These points define a concrete validation plan, rather than weaknesses that should be concealed.

\section{Conclusion}
This work presents a closed-loop framework for defect inspection on a circular knitting machine and \model, a compact direction- and content-adaptive detector. The workflow couples rotation-aware edge inspection, automatic local wired stop signaling, on-site operator inspection and troubleshooting, and a cloud digital twin for live status visualization, defect statistics, production-quality analysis, and authorized remote supervisory control. The detector combines DTE for horizontal, vertical, and local knitted-texture encoding with LTFR for adaptive feature-pyramid reconstruction. On the retained test split, \model improves YOLOv8n from 59.3\% to 66.3\% AP at 3.39 M parameters and 8.9 G FLOPs. The next step is not merely more offline accuracy: it is a disciplined site evaluation of sensing-to-stop latency, false-stop behavior, operator-troubleshooting workflow, production-quality-analysis value, and grouped generalization across production conditions.
