\begin{abstract}
Defect detection is essential for circular knitting production, where persistent needle or yarn abnormalities lead to repeated fabric defects, resulting in severe material waste and economic losses. However, existing studies are generally not tailored to circular knitting machines and tend to be cost-prohibitive, which hinders their large-scale industrial deployment. To address this issue, we first design and implement a low-cost, dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system for circular knitting machines. The system consists of a global-shutter camera, a Hall sensor, and a self-developed edge device. The Hall sensor signal triggers image acquisition and defect detection. When a defect is detected, the device stops the circular knitting machine to interrupt defect propagation and alerts the operator for corrective intervention. To further improve detection accuracy for diverse fabric defect morphologies, we propose a lightweight detector termed \model. To model the directional continuity of horizontal and vertical elongated defects, morphology-aware context encoding (MCE) augments backbone features with spatially extended directional context. To accommodate variations in defect morphology and scale, gated interpolation upsampling (GIU) adapts the reconstruction of higher-level context before fusion with local features, using a compact content-dependent gate. On the test set of a self-collected dataset from a real circular-knitting production line, \model achieves 97.2\% mAP50 and 65.5\% mAP50--95, improving on the YOLOv8n baseline by 6.7 and 7.1 percentage points, respectively, with a detection throughput of 22 FPS when deployed on the RK3566 edge device. These results demonstrate that the proposed system offers a cost-effective solution for large-scale deployment in real-time defect detection for circular-knitting production.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ircular} knitting machines are key production equipment for high-volume weft-knit fabric, which is widely used in everyday clothing such as T-shirts and sportswear \cite{cottonworksKnitBasics,woolmarkCircularKnitting,cottonworksSingleDouble}. The International Textile Manufacturers Federation reported approximately 24,500 global shipments of circular knitting machines in 2025 \cite{itmf2025press}. Globally, approximately 92 million tonnes of textile waste are generated annually, highlighting the importance of reducing avoidable material losses \cite{unep2025textilewaste}. Conventional quality checks are often performed after knitting is completed. A persistent needle or yarn abnormality can therefore generate repeated defects along a fabric roll before discovery, accumulating substantial fabric waste and economic losses \cite{takeuchi2018knitted,akter2022material,xiang2023hooknet}. To prevent such losses, real-time detection during knitting is needed to identify these defects early and enable timely intervention before further defective fabric is produced.

Edge computing offers a practical pathway toward such real-time detection, and has been increasingly adopted for industrial defect detection. In textile applications, Song et al. deployed EfficientDet-D0 on an NVIDIA Jetson TX2 and evaluated five fabric datasets covering dark red, grid, camouflage, digitally printed, and light blue fabrics\cite{song2021edgefabric}. Machado et al. deployed YOLO11n on a Jetson Orin Nano to detect solid-color cotton and linen fabrics on a fabric rolling line \cite{machado2025edgefabric}. Yan et al. deployed an improved YOLOv5s model on a Huawei Ascend 310-based Atlas platform to detect warp-knitted lace on warp-knitting machines \cite{yan2026warplace}.

Beyond textile detection, Wang et al. and Zhang et al. deployed an improved YOLOv5s model for rail surface defect detection and an improved Faster R-CNN detector with convolutional feature reuse for UAV-based insulator defect inspection, respectively, both on Jetson Xavier NX \cite{wang2024rail,zhang2026uavedge}. Li et al. deployed LiteYOLO-ID on a Jetson TX2 NX for insulator defect detection \cite{li2024liteyolo}, and Zhao et al. implemented KAM-Net on a Xilinx ZCU102 FPGA for glass defect segmentation \cite{zhao2025kamnet}. These studies predominantly adopt general-purpose edge-computing platforms or FPGA development boards. However, such platforms are not tailored to the sensing, control, and installation constraints of circular knitting machines. Their relatively high hardware cost, together with the additional integration effort required for image acquisition and machine control, hinders economically viable deployment across large numbers of machines.

In terms of detection algorithms, YOLO-based detectors have been widely explored for industrial defect detection because of their efficient inference and explicit defect localization \cite{redmon2016yolo}. Yang et al. developed YOLOV4-TinyS using lightweight convolutions, shallow-feature fusion, and attention, and evaluated the detector in a conveyor-based fabric detection system \cite{yang2024yolov4tinys}. Kang and Li proposed AYOLOv7-tiny for solid-color circular weft fabrics, combining space-to-depth downsampling with hybrid attention to preserve subtle defect information \cite{kang2024ayolov7}. Chen et al. enhanced YOLOv8 with Swin Transformer blocks, a bidirectional feature pyramid, attention, and a modified localization loss \cite{chen2025yolov8}. FA-YOLO combines receptive-field enhancement, attention, and adaptive spatial feature fusion \cite{yu2024fayolo}. DGHR-YOLO integrates deformable convolution for morphology adaptation with a high-level screening feature pyramid and lightweight feature processing \cite{zhang2025dghr}. Zhao et al. proposed attention-based multiscale feature fusion (AMFF), combining intralayer feature enhancement and cross-layer attention to strengthen weak defect representations on fabric and aluminum surfaces \cite{zhao2024amff}. An and Wan introduced JDCBL, combining contextual Transformer features with balanced contrastive learning to address scale variation and long-tailed defect categories in fabric inspection \cite{an2025jdcbl}. Wang et al. developed RRNet, a lightweight rotated fabric defect detector that combines position-guided fusion and multiscale rotation-guided strip coupling to model elongated and tilted defect structures \cite{wang2026rrnet}. Together, these methods advance the representation of subtle defects, irregular shapes, and scale variation. Related work on photovoltaic-cell and pipeline-weld detection also explores task-specific feature pyramids, contextual relations, and lightweight rotated representations \cite{su2022baf,shen2024crnet,liu2025lrnet}. However, these methods are tailored to their respective detection targets and may not be directly applicable to defect detection on circular knitting machines. Defects on circular knitting machines exhibit diverse morphologies and scales, ranging from horizontal and vertical elongated patterns to localized holes. In addition, modules such as deformable convolution and attention often introduce substantial computational overhead, making them less suitable for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices.

Motivated by these limitations, we design and implement a low-cost, dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system for circular knitting machines. The system integrates a power adapter, a global-shutter camera with LED illumination, a coaxial serializer/deserializer (SerDes) image-transmission link, a Hall sensor, and a self-developed edge device. The Hall sensor detects machine rotation to trigger image acquisition and defect detection. When a defect is detected, the edge device stops the circular knitting machine, uploads the defect image and record, and pushes an alert to the operator through the cloud platform for corrective intervention.

For defect detection within this system, we develop \model, a lightweight detector based on YOLOv8n that incorporates the morphology priors for horizontal and vertical elongated defects. MCE supplies spatially extended morphology-aware context while preserving the original local C2f feature paths. GIU addresses the differing reconstruction needs of elongated defects and holes of varying sizes by adaptively adjusting the interpolation of higher-level context before fusion with local lateral features. Together, the two components connect morphology-aware context encoding with spatial reconstruction within a compact parameter budget.

The main contributions of our work are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\arabic{enumi})}
  \item We design and implement a dedicated defect detection and closed-loop system for circular knitting machines, integrating image acquisition, camera transmission, edge inference, and local machine-stop control, and demonstrate its deployment on a real production line.
  \item We develop MCE to explicitly incorporate the morphology priors of horizontal and vertical elongated defects. It embeds spatially extended, morphology-aware context in a partial C2f branch while retaining the original local feature extraction paths with small additional overhead.
  \item We design GIU to accommodate the diverse morphologies and scales of circular-knitting defects, including elongated defects and holes of varying sizes. A compact spatial gate adapts a convex combination of two fixed interpolation paths, balancing response replication and neighborhood smoothing in the top-down path before fusion with local lateral features.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Defect Detection and Closed-Loop Control System}
\label{sec:system-design}
\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:system-overview}
We design and implement a dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system for circular knitting machines, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:interactions}. Our system integrates a power adapter, a global-shutter camera with LED illumination, a SerDes image-transmission link, a Hall sensor, a self-developed edge device, and a cloud platform. These components are organized into four functional subsystems: power supply, image acquisition and transmission, network communication, and processor-based local computation and control. The power subsystem supplies the device, which in turn powers and controls the LED illumination, and the image subsystem delivers fabric images to the processor over the SerDes link. The processor performs local defect detection and machine-stop control. The network subsystem uploads defect images and records to the cloud and delivers alert notifications to operator.

The complete closed-loop workflow begins when a operator starts the circular knitting machine. The Hall sensor detects machine rotation and sends a signal to the processor, which switches on the camera and LED illumination and enters the detection state. When a fabric defect is detected, the processor first sends a stop command to the machine controller to interrupt further defect propagation. The system then uploads the defect image and associated record to the cloud, which pushes an alert notification to inform the operator that intervention is required. The operator inspects the reported defect, resolves the production fault, and restarts the machine; the rotation signal then reactivates detection. This sequence connects production, rotation sensing, image acquisition, defect detection, machine stopping, defect reporting, operator notification, and corrective intervention into a complete production--detection--control loop. The following subsections describe the four subsystem designs, followed by their hardware implementation.

\subsection{Power Supply Subsystem}
To accommodate different industrial supply voltages across countries and regions, we design a power adapter with a 100--500 V AC input range and a 24 V DC output rated at 3 A. The adapter first supplies this output to the device. The device then supplies the LED illumination. By adjusting the current delivered to the LEDs, the device controls the illumination brightness to suit the image-acquisition conditions. This arrangement integrates LED power delivery and brightness control into the device.

\subsection{Image Acquisition and Transmission Subsystem}
The rapid rotation of circular knitting machines requires imaging that preserves defect geometry and fine spatial detail. We therefore select a  SmartSens SC130GS global-shutter sensor(SmartSens Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at $1280\times1024$ resolution, which exposes all pixels simultaneously to avoid motion-induced distortion from sequential row exposure. At a camera-to-fabric distance of approximately 10 cm, the field of view is approximately $16\times12$ cm, with the 16 cm dimension aligned with the horizontal direction of fabric motion.

Factory machinery creates an electromagnetically noisy environment, and installation requires the camera and edge device to be separated by a cable run. We therefore use serializer/deserializer (SerDes) transmission over shielded coaxial cable to accommodate remote camera placement and the interference environment. Specifically, the THCV241A serializer and THCV242A deserializer(THine Electronics, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) bridge the camera-side and processor-side MIPI CSI-2 interfaces over a V-by-One HS link. The devices support a maximum serial line rate of 4.0 Gb/s per lane, corresponding to a rated effective rate of 3.2 Gb/s \cite{thineTHCV241A,thineTHCV242A}.

The camera is configured to transmit $1280\times1024$ RAW10 images at 30 frames/s. With $b=10$ bits per pixel, the active-image payload rate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_{\mathrm{img}} &= W H b f_{\mathrm{cam}} \\
&=1280\times1024\times10\times30 \\
&=393.216\ \mathrm{Mb/s},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:serdes-bandwidth}
\end{equation}
where $W$ and $H$ denote the image width and height, and $f_{\mathrm{cam}}$ is the camera acquisition rate. This corresponds to 49.152 MB/s, accounting for only 12.3\% of the rated single-lane effective capacity, which provides a stable image input for the system's defect detection.

\subsection{Network Communication Subsystem}
The network communication hardware is integrated on the device and comprises a 4G module, a SIM-card slot, and an antenna connector for an external antenna. The 4G module provides the data link between the device and the cloud platform. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:interactions}, MQTT carries device-state and defect-event messages between the device and cloud, while an HTTP interface gives operators access to monitoring information and defect records. When the device detects a defect, it uploads the defect record and image to the cloud, which pushes a defect alert to the operator interface. The alert provides the defect category, occurrence time, and image for review and troubleshooting.

\subsection{Processor Subsystem}
\label{sec:coverage}
The processor is selected according to the detection rate required by fabric motion and the camera field of view. For a design diameter of 40 in ($D=1.016$ m) and a rotation period of $T=2$ s, the fabric travels approximately $\pi D=3.192$ m per revolution. With an effective field-of-view width of $w=0.16$ m along the direction of motion, the required frame rate is
\begin{equation}
f_{\min}=\frac{\pi D}{Tw}
=\frac{\pi\times1.016}{2\times0.16}
\approx9.97\ \mathrm{frames/s}.
\label{eq:coverage-rate}
\end{equation}
We set the effective detection-throughput requirement to 10 frames/s, corresponding to 20 processed frames per revolution. For this throughput target, we select the Rockchip RK3566(Rockchip Electronics Co., Ltd., Fuzhou, China) to balance processing capability, camera connectivity, and hardware cost. It integrates a quad-core Cortex-A55 CPU, an NPU rated at 1 TOPS for INT8 operations, and camera input interfaces \cite{rockchiprk3566}.

Table~\ref{tab:hardware-platforms} summarizes the hardware specifications of platforms used in related defect detection studies. While high-performance platforms like the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano and Xilinx ZCU102 offer substantial computational power, their costs range from hundreds to over 3,000 USD, which can be prohibitive for large-scale deployment. In contrast, our self-developed Rockchip RK3566 device is optimized for the target throughput of 10 frames/s. Although its rated compute capability is 1 TOPS (INT8), it offers a cost-effective hardware basis for the proposed edge detection system. Most notably, at a hardware cost of only 60 USD, our solution offers a significant cost advantage over comparable industrial platforms, making it highly suitable for cost-sensitive mass production environments.

\section{Detection Algorithm}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{MCGI-YOLO Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:network} shows \model based on YOLOv8n \cite{ultralytics2023}. Morphology-aware context encoding (MCE) is embedded in the P3 and P4 backbone C2f blocks to capture the continuity of horizontal and vertical elongated defects through spatially extended context. Gated interpolation upsampling (GIU) replaces the two top-down upsampling operations, adapting higher-level context reconstruction to different defect morphologies and scales before lateral feature fusion. The remaining backbone, bottom-up path, and detection head are retained \cite{lin2017fpn,liu2018panet}.

\label{fig:network}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Morphology-Aware Context Encoding}
Horizontal and vertical defects produced by circular knitting machines exhibit elongated morphologies, with a large difference between their longitudinal extent and transverse width. Their continuity is distributed over distant image regions, whereas their narrow profiles require local detail to remain distinguishable from the surrounding fabric texture. Local feature extraction alone does not explicitly relate these spatially separated responses as parts of the same defect. Meanwhile, introducing extensive feature interactions across all channels would increase the computational burden on the RK3566. We therefore develop MCE to incorporate the morphology prior of elongated defects through spatially extended context, while retaining local feature evidence within a compact computational budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}).

\emph{1) Placement and Channel Allocation:}
MCE is inserted after the last bottleneck in the P3 and P4 backbone C2f blocks, before branch concatenation and fusion [Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}(a)]. The original residual bottleneck is retained [Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}(c)]. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}(d), its input is $F\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times H\times W}$. The channels are divided into two groups: one quarter of the channels, denoted by $U$, are used for morphology-aware context aggregation and residual modulation; the remaining three quarters, denoted by $V$, bypass these operations and retain their locally extracted responses. This allocation confines the added context operations to a small channel subset while preserving local texture and spatial detail in the larger bypass group. The two groups are concatenated after modulation, allowing the subsequent fusion to combine morphology-aware and unmodulated local features. Applying MCE only at the branch end also avoids repeating context computation at each bottleneck. With $q=C/4$, the two groups have sizes $U\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times q\times H\times W}$ and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times(C-q)\times H\times W}$. The split preserves the batch and spatial dimensions.

\emph{2) Morphology-Aware Context Aggregation:}
The one-quarter-channel group $U$ feeds the two pooling paths shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}(d). Here, $b$, $a$, $h$, and $w$ index the batch sample, channel, row, and column, respectively. Following the directional pooling principle of coordinate attention \cite{hou2021coordinate}, we average independently for each sample and channel:
\begin{align}
R_h(b,a,h,1) &= \frac{1}{W}\sum_{w=1}^{W}U(b,a,h,w), \label{eq:pac-row}\\
R_w(b,a,1,w) &= \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}U(b,a,h,w). \label{eq:pac-column}
\end{align}
Averaging over $W$ gathers horizontally distributed evidence while retaining row positions; averaging over $H$ gathers vertically distributed evidence while retaining column positions. The resulting descriptors have sizes $B\times q\times H\times1$ and $B\times q\times1\times W$, respectively; the singleton dimension corresponds to the pooled spatial direction. They pass through $3\times1$ and $1\times3$ depthwise convolutions and are added by broadcasting:
\begin{equation}
T=\operatorname{DW}_{3\times1}(R_h)
 +\operatorname{DW}_{1\times3}(R_w).
\label{eq:pac-context}
\end{equation}
The filters refine variations between neighboring rows and columns. Padding preserves descriptor dimensions, and broadcasting produces $T\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times q\times H\times W}$. Long-range context is supplied by pooling over the full width and height, while the learned filters operate on the compressed descriptors.

\emph{3) Residual Context Modulation:}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}(d), the combined context $T$ is mapped by Hardsigmoid and centered to construct a scaled residual correction:
\begin{align}
G &= \operatorname{HS}(T), \label{eq:pac-map}\\
U' &= U+\gamma\odot U\odot(2G-1), \label{eq:pac-modulation}
\end{align}
where $\operatorname{HS}$ is Hardsigmoid, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication with broadcasting, and $\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times q\times1\times1}$ is a learnable channel-wise scale shared across batch samples and spatial positions. The context response $G\in[0,1]^{B\times q\times H\times W}$ is centered by $2G-1$ to provide signed modulation, with $\gamma$ controlling its channel-wise strength. The original $U$ is retained through the residual connection, and the corrected one-quarter-channel group $U'$ is concatenated with the unchanged three-quarter-channel group $V$. The corrected group has size $U'\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times q\times H\times W}$, and channel concatenation produces a $B\times C\times H\times W$ output before the original C2f fusion. Zero-initializing $\gamma$ makes the module an identity mapping at initialization. The two biased depthwise filters and residual scales add $9q$ parameters.

\subsection{Gated Interpolation Upsampling}
Circular-knitting defects vary substantially in morphology and scale, from narrow horizontal and vertical patterns to small and large holes. Their feature representations therefore place different demands on spatial continuity, response concentration, and boundary extent. In the top-down feature pyramid, higher-level context must be reconstructed at a finer resolution before fusion with lateral features. Nearest-neighbor interpolation replicates responses within local regions, whereas bilinear interpolation combines neighboring responses to produce smoother spatial transitions. A fixed interpolation rule applies the same reconstruction behavior to all locations, limiting its adaptability to these diverse defect patterns. We develop GIU to adjust the balance between response replication and neighborhood smoothing according to the input features [Fig.~\ref{fig:modules}(b)]. To achieve this adaptation with a small computational overhead, GIU combines two parameter-free interpolation paths using a single spatial weight map predicted at low resolution and shared across channels. It reconstructs the higher-level context before fusion with lateral features $F_3$ and $F_4$, which retain local texture and localization cues.

\emph{1) Two Fixed Interpolation Paths:}
For $X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times H\times W}$, nearest-neighbor and bilinear upsampling produce
\begin{align}
N &= \operatorname{Nearest}_{2}(X), \label{eq:giu-paths}\\
L &= \operatorname{Bilinear}_{2}(X), \label{eq:giu-bilinear}
\end{align}
where $N,L\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times2H\times2W}$ and bilinear interpolation uses \texttt{align\_corners=False}. The two paths provide complementary reconstruction behaviors without introducing learned interpolation parameters.

\emph{2) Shared Spatial Gate:}
A $3\times3$ convolution followed by Hardsigmoid predicts a low-resolution spatial weight map, which is then upsampled by nearest-neighbor interpolation:
\begin{align}
g &= \operatorname{HS}(W_g*X+b_g), \label{eq:giu-gate-lowres}\\
G &= \operatorname{Nearest}_{2}(g), \label{eq:giu-gate}
\end{align}
where $g\in[0,1]^{B\times1\times H\times W}$ is the low-resolution weight map, $*$ denotes convolution, $W_g\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C\times3\times3}$, and $b_g$ is a scalar bias. Padding of one preserves spatial dimensions. The map $G\in[0,1]^{B\times1\times2H\times2W}$ assigns a content-dependent interpolation weight to each spatial location and is broadcast across feature channels. Predicting this map at $H\times W$ limits the spatial cost of the learned convolution, while channel sharing requires only one output weight per location.

\emph{3) Gated Reconstruction:}
The output $Y\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times2H\times2W}$ is a convex combination:
\begin{equation}
Y=(1-G)\odot N+G\odot L.
\label{eq:giu-mixture}
\end{equation}
Small values of $G$ favor response replication, while larger values favor neighborhood smoothing. This spatially varying mixture allows the reconstruction behavior to adapt to local feature content associated with different defect morphologies and scales. Initializing $W_g=0$ and $b_g=-2.7$ gives
\begin{equation}
G_{\mathrm{init}}=0.05,\qquad Y_{\mathrm{init}}=0.95N+0.05L,
\label{eq:giu-initialization}
\end{equation}
so the module starts from a predominantly nearest-neighbor reconstruction, consistent with the baseline top-down pathway.

Each GIU adds $9C+1$ parameters, with $9CHW$ multiply--accumulate operations per image for the gate convolution. The fixed interpolation paths, low-resolution weight prediction, and channel sharing concentrate the learned computation in a single compact convolution; the remaining reconstruction uses interpolation and element-wise mixing. This design provides content-adaptive upsampling within the computational budget of the RK3566. The individual and combined module effects are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:component-ablation}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}
\subsection{Dataset}
Images were collected during operation of a real circular-knitting production line. A global-shutter industrial camera captures $1280\times1024$ frames approximately 10 cm from the fabric, with a field of view of approximately $16\times12$ cm, where the 16 cm dimension follows the circumferential motion. We selected images with visible defects and manually annotated their bounding boxes. The dataset includes three defect categories: horizontal defects (\emph{row}), vertical defects (\emph{col}), and holes (\emph{hole}).

The dataset contains 403 images with 468 annotated defect instances, divided into training, validation, and test sets of 282, 40, and 81 images, respectively, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:data}. The validation set is used for checkpoint selection, while performance is reported on the test set containing 91 labeled defects. Because the selected images contain visible defects, this test set evaluates defect localization and classification; it does not measure false-stop frequency during continuous detection of normal fabric.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation}
Intersection over union (IoU) measures the overlap between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes as the ratio of their intersection area to their union area. Detection accuracy is evaluated using mAP50 and mAP50--95 \cite{lin2014coco}. Let $a_c(t)$ denote the average precision for class $c$ at IoU threshold $t$. The metrics are computed as
\begin{align}
\operatorname{mAP}_{50} &= \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}a_c(0.50),
\label{eq:metrics}\\
\operatorname{mAP}_{50:95} &= \frac{1}{10N_c}\sum_{c=1}^{N_c}\sum_{t\in T}a_c(t),
\label{eq:ap}
\end{align}
where $N_c=3$ and $T=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\}$. Both metrics are reported as percentages, with improvements expressed in percentage points.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Comparison With Other Detectors}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} compares test-set accuracy, model complexity, and inference throughput. \model achieves the highest mAP50 of 97.2\% and the second-highest mAP50--95 of 65.5\%, improving over the YOLOv8n baseline by 6.7 and 7.1 percentage points, respectively. This gain comes with only a marginal increase in parameters (3.01 M $\rightarrow$ 3.02 M) and FLOPs (8.1 G $\rightarrow$ 8.2 G), while throughput on the RTX 3090 decreases from 554 to 486 FPS. Among lightweight variants, YOLO11n attains the next-best accuracy (93.3\% mAP50, 60.8\% mAP50--95); \model surpasses it by 3.9 and 4.7 points while running faster (486 vs.\ 463 FPS). Several other variants have fewer parameters or lower FLOPs but underperform on this test set, confirming that task-specific adaptation outperforms generic lightweight detectors. RT-DETR-R18 leads in mAP50--95 (67.7\%) but requires 20.09 M parameters and 60.7 G FLOPs at 146 FPS. \model also outperforms DETR-R50, Deformable DETR-R50, and Faster R-CNN-R50 on both mAP metrics with substantially lower cost, offering the best overall accuracy--efficiency tradeoff. Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows that \model produces fewer fragmented boxes and tighter localization on elongated defects and holes, consistent with the aggregate results.

\subsection{Model Deployment and On-Device Performance}
\label{sec:deployment}
To deploy \model on the dedicated edge device, the trained model is exported to ONNX with a fixed input size of $640\times$640 and then converted into an INT8 RKNN model using RKNN-Toolkit2 \cite{rockchiptoolkit2}. For $640\times$640 inputs, the deployed INT8 model achieves a measured inference throughput of 22 FPS on the RK3566. This throughput is 2.2 times the 10 FPS design target derived in Section~\ref{sec:coverage}, meeting the model-inference rate required for the specified machine speed and camera field of view.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presents a dedicated defect detection and closed-loop control system for circular knitting machines, integrating image acquisition, SerDes transmission, edge inference, machine stopping, and cloud alerts. The lightweight \model combines MCE for elongated-defect morphology encoding with GIU for adaptive feature reconstruction. On the self-collected test set, it achieves 97.2\% mAP50 and 65.5\% mAP50--95, improving over YOLOv8n by 6.7 and 7.1 percentage points. INT8 deployment on the RK3566 achieves 22 FPS, exceeding the 10 FPS design target. Future work will evaluate quantization-induced accuracy changes, end-to-end stopping performance, and false-stop frequency across fabric textures and operating conditions.
